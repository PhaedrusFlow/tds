```text
   2 | Introduction
   3 | Clinician scholar-educators follow a career path distinct from the traditional physician-
   4 | scientist model. Careers are built through teaching, mentorship, educational
   5 | scholarship, and program development. Yet the impact of this work can be harder to
   6 | measure than grants or manuscripts, and the rules of success are rarely explicitly
   7 | taught(1).
   8 | We came to this work from different backgrounds but with a shared experience of
   9 | learning those rules late. One of us is the daughter of first-generation South Asian
  10 | immigrants, raised to believe that hard work, humility, and service would speak for
  11 | themselves. Both of us found self-advocacy and asking for opportunities far less
  12 | intuitive than simply doing the work. Those values served us well, but they left us
  13 | unprepared for an academic culture that rewards visibility, sponsorship, and the ability
  14 | to articulate impact.
  15 | Having progressed from mentees to mentors, this perspective offers three lessons we
  16 | wish someone had told us, each addressed to mentors, who can make the hidden
  17 | curriculum explicit, and to mentees, who deserve to hear these lessons now rather than
  18 | spend a decade discovering it themselves.
```

```text
   1 | 3

   2 | Early in our careers, we thought success would be found in keeping our interests broad.
   3 | We eventually learned that clinician scholar-educators build influence through depth. A
   4 | niche is the intersection of expertise, an educational problem worth solving, and
   5 | sustained contribution.  Over time, repetition becomes reputation, giving colleagues
   6 | clarity about what they seek your expertise for.
   7 | One of us found that niche in osteoporosis, not because it was glamorous or the sexiest
   8 | disease, but because it mattered to us. Over more than a decade, that commitment
   9 | grew into multidisciplinary clinical programs, educational innovation, quality
  10 | improvement, and national teaching.  We did not plan to become "the osteoporosis
  11 | educator." We kept returning to the same problem until colleagues began associating us
  12 | with it.

  13 | Being one of the few clinician educators at the intersection of a specialty and medical
  14 | education can feel isolating. Often, however, that isolation is the beginning of a niche.
  15 | Keep investing in the work that continues to energize you. Depth builds credibility, and
  16 | credibility creates opportunity.
  17 | A second part of this rule: reusing your work is not laziness. It is how knowledge scales.
  18 | The CME lecture you first gave in 2021, updated annually for new audiences, is not the
  19 | same lecture—it is an evolving body of educational scholarship. Physician-scientists
  20 | advance knowledge through discovery; clinician educators advance it through
  21 | refinement, dissemination, and implementation. See Figure 1.
```

```text
   1 | 4

   2 | To Mentors: Help your mentees identify the educational problem that energizes them and that
   3 | they keep returning to. Protect time for them to develop it and sponsor them to teach before
   4 | they have an extensive track record. One invitation can launch a reputation.
   5 | To Mentees: You do not need a new idea every year. You need one idea you pursue with more
   6 | depth and in more places than feels comfortable. Update last year’s lecture and make it ten
   7 | percent better. Turn it into a workshop, a chalk talk,or book chapter. Repetition is not lack of
   8 | creativity; it is how expertise becomes recognizable. If repetition feels boring, adapt and iterate.
   9 | Rule 2
  10 | Waiting to be noticed is a strategy. It just isn't a very effective one.
  11 | We believed hard work would speak for itself.  For many first-generation, immigrant, or
  12 | underrepresented faculty, that belief carries us far, but not far enough.
  13 | Many academic opportunities—committee appointments, invited talks, collaborations,
  14 | and leadership roles—arise through sponsorship. Mentors offer guidance; sponsors use
  15 | their credibility to create opportunities (2,3,4).
  16 | Like many introverts, asking felt unnatural.  One email sat in the draft folder for days. It
  17 | was rewritten four times because it felt presumptuous.  Looking back, the request itself
  18 | was never the problem. Sponsors cannot advocate for goals they do not know you
  19 | have.
  20 | For introverts, and for those from cultures where directness feels transgressive, this
  21 | bears repeating: asking takes energy, and that cost is real, not imagined. Draft the email
  22 | slowly. Practice the conversation. Ask for feedback. What you cannot do is skip the ask.
```

```text
   9 | Rule 3
  10 | A CV doesn't narrate your impact, it will always undersell you.
  11 | Academic promotion systems were largely designed around the physician-scientist
  12 | model, where grants, publications, and citations are readily measured. Clinician scholar-
  13 | educators create impact differently, and too often we are never taught how to describe
  14 | that work.

  16 | Yet that resident may mentor dozens more.  Likewise, a CME lecture may change how

  18 | learners. A culture you build where trainees feel safe asking for help can transform the

  21 | practice. The challenge is not the value of this work but our failure to describe it in the
```

```text
   1 | 6

   2 | into evidence: Who was reached? What changed? How did it spread? Why did it
   3 | matter? (5, 6)
   4 | Recognize achievements for what they are. Being appointed to a national committee is
   5 | not simply another line on your CV—it is evidence that your peers recognized your
   6 | expertise. Say so.
   7 | During promotion, a mentor helped one of us translate years of educational work into
   8 | language promotion committees could understand. Until that moment, it had never
   9 | occurred to us that this was something that could be done, and certainly not something
  10 | that we could ask for help doing.
  11 | To Mentors: Read teaching portfolios with the same attention you give publication lists.
  12 | Teach mentees how to translate educational work into evidence of impact.
  13 | To Mentees: Do not minimize your contributions. Replace "gave several lectures" with
  14 | language that explains who was reached, what changed, and why it mattered. If you do
  15 | not tell the story of your impact, no one else will know it’s there.
  16 | Conclusion:
  17 | These three rules are not a complete roadmap. These are simply the lessons we wish
  18 | someone had shared earlier. The hidden curriculum of clinician scholar-educators
  19 | should not depend on luck, proximity to influential mentors, or years of trial and error.
  20 | Making these lessons explicit is one of our greatest opportunities to strengthen the next
  21 | generation.
```
