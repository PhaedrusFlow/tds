## Confidential Comments to the Editor

In its current form this manuscript is not ready for publication, and the deficiencies are not confined to one section. The submission record misspells the manuscript's own short title ("What Noboody Told Me"). Two of the three rules that give the essay its structure are never titled in their headings. Neither figure was present in evaluable form in the review bundle (each appears as a caption plus a download pointer to a separate FIGURES.docx), and Figure 1's caption promises a four-checkpoint rubric that the text reduces to three components. Two of the six references are incomplete as printed, one with the DOI field blank. The abstract's description of the third rule does not match the rule the text delivers, and the first author's standpoint is described inconsistently between the abstract/cover letter ("first-generation immigrant") and the Introduction ("daughter of first-generation immigrants").

Individually, each defect is small. Collectively, they indicate a manuscript that was not proofread or verified before submission, and the argument has the same character: at each point where a claim widens beyond the authors' own experience — to first-generation, immigrant, and underrepresented faculty generally — evidence gives way to assertion and sentiment. The topic fits the journal and the "To Mentors / To Mentees" guidance structure is a sound idea worth salvaging, which is why I recommend rejection with resubmission invited rather than rejection outright: a rebuilt manuscript that was checked with the care this one was not could be publishable. I would be willing to review a resubmission. The editor may also wish to confirm whether the figure files were ever received by the editorial office.

## General Comments to Authors

The manuscript sets out to make the hidden curriculum of the clinician scholar-educator career explicit, as three rules drawn from the authors' experience, each closing with guidance for mentors and mentees. The topic belongs in this journal, and the closing guidance sections are a sound structural idea. The osteoporosis narrative in Rule 1 shows what the essay could be: specific, concrete, and earned.

As submitted, however, the manuscript is lacking — in production care, in scholarly apparatus, and in evidentiary support — and the three failures reinforce one another. The production record speaks first: the short title is misspelled in the submission record; two of three rule headings carry no title; the figures the text directs readers to are not in the manuscript; the affiliation block is garbled; and two references cannot be resolved as printed. The scholarly apparatus is thinner than the argument requires: the paper's central construct, the hidden curriculum, is never cited to its source literature, and the one Introduction citation is attached to a claim it does not support. And where the essay's claims extend beyond the authors' two careers — to first-generation, immigrant, and underrepresented faculty as populations — it offers feeling in place of the evidence that exists in its own reference list. An essay asking readers to trust its testimony must first demonstrate that it has been careful with everything checkable. This one has not been.

The comments below are ordered by consequence and are specific enough to rebuild against. None requires new data. All require the authors to apply to the manuscript the same diligence the manuscript recommends to its readers.

## Comment 1: manuscript pp. 9–10 (Figures) and p. 3 | line 57 — The figures are not present in evaluable form, and Figure 1 promises content the text never delivers

In the bundle as provided for review, neither figure appears. Figure 1 is represented by a caption ("Figure 1: Rule #1 — The Niche Rubric. Four checkpoints for building a teaching identity that holds up over time") followed by a download pointer to a separate file (FIGURES.docx), and Figure 2 by a caption alone. A reviewer therefore cannot verify the rubric, the figure legends, or whether the figures add information beyond the text.

The gap matters substantively, not just procedurally. The caption promises **four** checkpoints, but the text of Rule 1 (manuscript p. 3) defines a niche as the intersection of **three** components: expertise, an educational problem worth solving, and sustained contribution. Either the rubric's fourth checkpoint exists only in the figure — in which case the argument is incomplete in the text and inaccessible to print readers who skim — or the caption miscounts. Please embed both figures in the manuscript file, and state all four checkpoints in the text of Rule 1 so the rubric and the prose agree.

## Comment 2: manuscript p. 4 | line 22; p. 5 | line 25 — Rules 2 and 3 have no titles; the essay's central device is only one-third executed

Rule 1 carries its full title as a heading ("Rule 1: Your Niche Is Your Currency, Not Your Limitation"). The other two sections are headed "Rule 2" and "Rule 3" with no title at all, followed by an aphorism in deck position ("Waiting to be noticed is a strategy…" and "A CV doesn't narrate your impact…"). Figure 2's caption reveals that Rule 2 does have a title — "Sponsorship Doesn't Find You — You Have to Ask For It" — but it never appears at the section it names. Rule 3's title exists nowhere in the manuscript.

The abstract and title promise readers three named rules; a reader navigating by headings, or citing this piece, currently cannot name two of them. Please give Rules 2 and 3 headings in the same form as Rule 1, using the Figure 2 title for Rule 2 and a title for Rule 3 that states its actual claim (see Comment 4).

## Comment 3: manuscript p. 2 | line 20 — The paper's central construct, the hidden curriculum, is never anchored to its source literature, and reference 1 is attached to a claim it does not support

"Hidden curriculum" is the manuscript's premise, appears in the abstract and keywords, and carries the conclusion — yet the foundational literature for the construct (Hafferty's work on the hidden curriculum in medical education) is never cited. The single citation in the Introduction, reference 1, is attached to the claim that "the rules of success are rarely explicitly taught(1)." Reference 1 (Ryan et al.) is a survey of promotion and tenure committee members about how clinician-educators are *evaluated* for educational excellence. It documents the evaluation problem from the committee's side; it does not examine whether or how career rules are taught. The citation is adjacent to the claim, not evidence for it.

Please cite the hidden-curriculum source literature where the construct is introduced, and reposition reference 1 to a claim it supports — for example, the Introduction's preceding sentence about the impact of educator work being harder to measure than grants or manuscripts, which is exactly Ryan et al.'s subject.

**Relevant literature:**

- Ryan MS, Tucker C, DiazGranados D, Chandran L. How are clinician-educators evaluated for educational excellence? A survey of promotion and tenure committee members in the United States. Med Teach. 2019;41(8):927-933. doi: 10.1080/0142159X.2019.1596237 (verified against the published record).

## Comment 4: Abstract; manuscript pp. 5–6 — The abstract's third rule is not the rule the text delivers

The abstract states the third rule as: "relational work is scholarly work, and must be narrated as such." The first clause promises an argument that relational work — mentoring, culture-building, the personal-statement rewrite described on p. 5 — counts as scholarship. The text of Rule 3 delivers a different, narrower lesson: how to narrate *one's own* impact in the language of promotion ("Who was reached? What changed? How did it spread? Why did it matter?", p. 6, line 4). The relational examples are present, but they function as illustrations of unnarrated work, not as a defended claim that relational work is scholarly work; the actionable content is CV and portfolio phrasing, supported by references 5 and 6.

Either deliver the abstract's argument — a paragraph making the scholarly case for relational work itself would do it — or revise the abstract's third rule to match the delivered one. As written, a reader who comes for the abstract's promise will find a useful but different lesson.

## Comment 5: manuscript p. 4 | line 29 — Population-level claims about first-generation, immigrant, and underrepresented faculty are asserted without support

The manuscript moves between two registers: testimony ("One of us is the daughter of first-generation South Asian immigrants…", p. 2, line 26) and population claim ("For many first-generation, immigrant, or underrepresented faculty, that belief carries us far, but not far enough", p. 4, line 29; see also the cover letter's equity framing). Testimony needs no citation and is the essay's strength. Population claims do, because they will be quoted as findings. The disparities the authors invoke — differential access to sponsorship and to self-advocacy norms — are documented in the literature, including in the qualitative sponsorship data of the authors' own reference 3 (Ayyala et al.), whose protégés and sponsors describe exactly this asymmetry. Please either scope the generalizations explicitly to the authors' experience or support them where they are made; the essay's authority on this point should rest on more than its two cases, and the supporting literature is already in the reference list.

**Relevant literature:**

- Ayyala MS, Skarupski K, Bodurtha JN, et al. Mentorship Is Not Enough: Exploring Sponsorship and Its Role in Career Advancement in Academic Medicine. Acad Med. 2019;94(1):94-100. doi: 10.1097/ACM.0000000000002398 (verified against the published record).

## Comment 6: Abstract and cover letter vs manuscript p. 2 | line 26 — The first author's immigration framing is internally inconsistent

The abstract describes the perspective as written "from lived experience as a first-generation immigrant and introverts," and the cover letter repeats "navigating academic medicine as a first-generation immigrant." The Introduction, however, states: "One of us is the daughter of first-generation South Asian immigrants" (p. 2, line 26). A daughter of first-generation immigrants is ordinarily described as second-generation; "first-generation immigrant" and "daughter of first-generation immigrants" are different positions, and the distinction is not pedantic in an essay whose authority derives from the authors' standpoint and whose subject includes first-generation experience. Please reconcile the three statements so the standpoint the essay claims is the one it describes.

## Comment 7: References, manuscript pp. 7–8 — Two of six references are incomplete as printed, and one is positioned where it cannot support the claim

1. **Reference 2** (Chopra V, Edelson DP, Saint S. "A piece of my mind: mentorship malpractice." JAMA, 2016) omits volume, pages, and DOI. The essay is readily locatable — Chopra V, Edelson DP, Saint S. Mentorship Malpractice. JAMA. 2016 (A Piece of My Mind, April 12, 2016) — and the full details should be supplied. Separately, its placement deserves attention: it is cited, with references 3 and 4, for the distinction that "Mentors offer guidance; sponsors use their credibility to create opportunities" (manuscript p. 4). "Mentorship Malpractice" is a taxonomy of *dysfunctional* mentorship; the distinction the sentence draws rests on references 3 and 4. Either reposition reference 2 to a claim about mentorship's failure modes or state explicitly what it contributes to the distinction.
2. **Reference 6** (Wong DH, Lui JK, Benjamin EJ. Integrating coaching activities into the educator portfolio. Acad Med. 2026) is printed with the DOI field empty and only a partial identifier ("wvag108"); as printed, a reader cannot resolve it. Please supply the complete DOI and publication details.
3. **Reference 4** (Cruz EB, et al. J Gen Intern Med. 2026) is an epub-ahead-of-print citation in a three-part JGIM series on promotion; please re-verify its date and identifiers against the version of record at revision, since ahead-of-print details routinely change.

## Suggested Editorial Disposition

**Reject — resubmission of a substantially revised manuscript may be considered.**

1. The manuscript's topic, length, and guidance structure are appropriate for JGME Perspectives, and Rule 1's narrative demonstrates the authors can write the essay they intended. Rejection outright, without a path back, would be disproportionate.
2. But the submission as it stands falls below the standard for revision-in-place. A major-revision decision implies a sound manuscript with localized defects; here the defects reach every level of the piece — submission metadata (a misspelled short title), section architecture (two of three rules untitled), evidence presentation (both figures absent; a rubric whose stated contents the text contradicts), scholarly anchoring (the central construct uncited; the Introduction's citation misattached), framing (abstract and text describing different third rules; the author's standpoint described two incompatible ways), and the reference list (two of six entries unresolvable as printed). Correcting them amounts to rebuilding the manuscript, which is resubmission work, not revision work.
3. The argumentative standard points the same way. A Perspectives essay trades on the credibility of its testimony. Where this manuscript generalizes — about first-generation, immigrant, and underrepresented faculty — it asserts rather than supports, and its emotional register is asked to carry weight that evidence should carry. Fairness to the journal's readers requires holding a testimony-driven piece to a *higher* standard of care in everything verifiable, and this submission does not meet it.
4. A resubmission should: embed both figures and reconcile Figure 1's rubric with the text; title all three rule headings; anchor the hidden-curriculum framing in its source literature and reposition reference 1; bring the abstract and Rule 3 into agreement; scope or support the population-level claims; reconcile the standpoint descriptions; complete references 2 and 6; and be proofread end to end, including the submission metadata. I would be willing to review such a resubmission on its merits.
