   1 | Introduction
   2 | Writing a first scientific paper is a transition from doing research to communicating an argument that
   3 | can survive editorial and peer scrutiny. Beginner-oriented guides have made this transition more
   4 | approachable by explaining IMRaD, providing section-by-section advice, and converting publication into
   5 | manageable steps.1,2,3 Recent primers continue to address the same educational gap among residents
   6 | and inexperienced authors, suggesting that scientific writing remains inconsistently taught despite its
   7 | importance to academic development.4,5
   8 | The problem is no longer a lack of instructions. The problem is fragmentation. A novice may know what
   9 | belongs in a Discussion yet still struggle with the decisions that make the entire manuscript coherent:
  10 | What is the single defensible message? Which results deserve a table or figure? Which reporting
  11 | guideline should shape the manuscript? What should be drafted first? How can the author verify that
  12 | each number and citation is trustworthy? Scientific-storytelling work similarly argues that compliance
  13 | with IMRaD does not, by itself, create a coherent scientific narrative.6
  14 | The contemporary publication environment adds two pressures. First, reporting guidelines are intended
  15 | to improve completeness and transparency and are most useful when incorporated while drafting rather
  16 | than discovered at the end.7 Second, generative AI can assist with language and organization but can
  17 | also produce incorrect, incomplete, biased, or unattributed content. Updated ICMJE guidance makes
  18 | human authors responsible for AI-assisted material and requires transparent disclosure of its use.8,9

   4 | objective, displays, Results, and Discussion should converge on the same scientific claim. If they do

   9 | Reporting guidelines should be identified before prose is mature. EQUATOR describes reporting
  10 | guidelines as tools that help authors include important information and improve completeness and
  11 | transparency.7 Depending on study design, examples include CONSORT, STROBE, PRISMA, STARD,
  12 | CARE, and TRIPOD.
  13 | We recommend that novice authors then build an “evidence skeleton”: the minimum set of tables and
  14 | figures needed to answer the study objectives. Each display should have a defined job. If a table does
  15 | not answer an objective or support the central message, it may be secondary, supplementary, or
  16 | unnecessary. This step exposes missing analyses and contradictions before they are hidden inside
  17 | polished prose.
  18 | WRITE: Read in IMRaD; Draft Around the Evidence
  19 | IMRaD remains the dominant reading architecture of original biomedical research, but drafting does not
  20 | need to follow the reading order. Section-level writing guidance emphasizes purposeful construction of
  21 | the Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion, while scientific-storytelling approaches encourage

   2 | Verification should be deliberate, not assumed to occur during proofreading. We recommend four
   3 | audits: (1) numerical consistency across abstract, text, tables, figures, and supplements; (2) analytic
   4 | consistency between stated methods and reported results; (3) citation fidelity—opening the primary

   6 | applicable guideline. Verification may send the author back to THINK, STRUCTURE, or WRITE; that is

  10 | Discussion
  11 | The main contribution of this review is a change in unit of instruction. Existing literature already teaches
  12 | sections, steps, and submission logistics well. We propose teaching the first manuscript as a series of
  13 | decisions that must remain aligned from question to publication. This framing may help residents
  14 | recognize why a paper feels “stuck”: the problem may be an unstable message (THINK), incomplete
  15 | reporting or displays (STRUCTURE), section-level execution (WRITE), unverified claims (VERIFY), or
  16 | journal mismatch (PUBLISH).
  17 | The framework also makes explicit a distinction that is increasingly important in the generative-AI era:
  18 | assistance is not authorship, and fluency is not validity. A polished paragraph can still be scientifically
  19 | wrong. By making VERIFY a named stage, the model treats factual and citation checking as scholarly
  20 | work rather than clerical cleanup. This is consistent with contemporary editorial expectations for human
  21 | accountability and disclosure.
  22 | For graduate medical education, the framework is intended to support—not replace—mentorship.
  23 | Residents often write under clinical time pressure and may have uneven access to experienced
  24 | research mentors. A shared five-stage vocabulary can make supervision more efficient: instead of
  25 | “improve the paper,” an educator can identify the decision that requires attention. The model may also
  26 | be used to structure workshops, longitudinal writing curricula, or peer-writing groups across specialties.
  27 | The evidence base warrants caution. Authoritative standards strongly support transparent reporting,
  28 | human responsibility, and verification, but the optimal order for drafting manuscript sections has not
  29 | been established experimentally. The proposed writing sequence is therefore a teaching heuristic.
  30 | Similarly, the framework itself has not yet been prospectively evaluated. A logical next step is to test it in

  32 | manuscript quality scored by blinded raters, writing self-efficacy, time to submission, reviewer-
