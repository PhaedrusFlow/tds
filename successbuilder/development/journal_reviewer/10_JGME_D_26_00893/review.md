## Confidential Comments to the Editor

This is a competent, honestly hedged narrative review that proposes a five-stage workflow (THINK–STRUCTURE–WRITE–VERIFY–PUBLISH) for first-time resident authors, and it has real strengths: VERIFY as a named stage is a genuine contribution, the generative-AI disclosure is complete and consistent between the manuscript and the cover letter, and the author is unusually candid that the framework is an unevaluated teaching heuristic rather than a validated guideline. I nevertheless cannot recommend acceptance in current form, for three reasons the authors can fix. First, the paper's central branding is not delivered: it calls itself "decision-based" and claims to supply a "decision architecture," but at essentially every point where a decision is named, the criterion for making it is left to unaided judgment — there are no thresholds, no branching rules, no checkable gate criteria, and no worked example applying the framework to a single manuscript end to end (see the Determinism Audit below). Second, and more concerning for a paper whose thesis is verification: reference 6 — the citation that carries the THINK stage's scientific-storytelling rationale and is cited again for the WRITE drafting order — cannot be verified as cited. The DOI does not resolve in Crossref or at doi.org, no such article appears in PubMed, and the entry appears to conflate two real sources (the title of Montagnes et al., *Marine Life Science & Technology* 2021, with the author pair Mensh & Kording, whose story-structuring paper is *PLoS Computational Biology* 2017). The manuscript's own disclosure states that the author "independently verified cited sources," and Box 1 instructs readers that "every important citation was opened and checked against the original source"; the editor should require a line-by-line re-verification of all 19 references as a condition of revision (my audit of the remaining references is in the Reference Audit below: 17 verified clean, 1 partially verified). Third, the policy synthesis omits the COPE position statement on AI and authorship and never engages JGME's own 2026 editorial guidance on AI use and disclosure — the two sources most operative for this journal's readers. None of this suggests misconduct; the reference defect has the signature of an AI-assisted drafting error that survived the author's stated verification, which is itself relevant information for the editor given the paper's subject. With a worked example, checkable gate criteria, a repaired and re-verified reference list, and engagement with COPE and JGME's own AI policy, this could become a useful JGME review. Without those, it is a well-written set of prompts wearing a framework's title.

## General Comments to Authors

Thank you for a clearly written and unusually honest manuscript. The problem you identify is real: residents are taught the anatomy of a paper (IMRaD) far more reliably than they are taught the judgments that connect its parts, and your proposal to reorganize instruction around those judgments is sensible. Several elements deserve to survive revision intact. Making VERIFY a named, separate stage — rather than assuming verification happens during proofreading — is the paper's best idea, and the four audits (p. 10 L.1–7) are concrete enough to teach. The traffic-light model for generative AI (Table 2) is the most immediately usable element in the paper. Table 3's positioning against prior guidance is candid about what is borrowed and what is added. Your hedging is exemplary: the drafting sequence is "not claimed to be superior" (p. 8 L.23–26), the framework is "an evidence-informed educational synthesis requiring prospective evaluation, not a prescriptive guideline" (p. 7 L.1–5), and the Discussion names the right evaluation outcomes (p. 12 L.30–33). The generative-AI disclosure is complete, specific, and consistent between the manuscript (p. 13 L.17–20) and the cover letter.

The difficulties are threefold, and they are the subject of the numbered comments and the two audits that follow. First, the manuscript promises decisions and delivers prompts: the gap between the "decision-based" branding and the judgment-dependent content is the paper's central weakness, and it is fixable. Second, the Methods report the search at a level that cannot be reproduced or appraised against the SANRA standard the paper invokes, and the evidentiary base for the educational claims (references 17 and 18) is thinner and further from residents than the framing suggests. Third, the reference list contains one citation that cannot be verified as printed, at a load-bearing point, alongside several canonical omissions (COPE; JGME's own AI guidance; the reporting-guideline statements the STRUCTURE stage names). Page and line citations below refer to the review PDF; PDF pages 4–14 carry manuscript folios 1–11. In the version provided for review, Figures 1 and 2 appear as placeholders, so comments on the framework rest on the text and tables; if the figures carry operational content the text lacks, some comments below may need revisiting.

## Comment 1 — Title; p. 7 L.14–18; p. 8 L.21–26; p. 9 L.3–62 (Table 1); p. 11 L.50–54 and p. 12 L.1–9 (Box 1)

The manuscript's title and framing promise a "decision-based framework" and a "compact decision architecture" (p. 7 L.16), but the content supplies decision *questions*, not decision *rules*. I inventoried every stage and every place the paper claims a decision is made (full inventory in the Determinism Audit below). The pattern is uniform: inputs are loosely defined, criteria are qualitative, there is no branching logic, and the stopping conditions are judgments restated as gates. Table 1's "Move on when…" column is the clearest example. One of its five gates is genuinely checkable ("the displays and text agree," p. 9 L.16–19), but the Discussion gate — "interpretation is proportional to design, effect size, uncertainty, and prior evidence" (p. 9 L.38–41) — names the four things a judge should weigh without giving the novice any way to weigh them, and the Abstract/Title gate ("they faithfully represent the completed paper," p. 9 L.59–61) is circular. A resident who cannot yet judge proportionality is precisely the reader this paper is for; a gate she cannot apply does not gate anything. The same applies to Box 1, whose seven items are self-attestations ("I can state…," "every important citation was opened and checked") with no external check, no scoring, and no consequence for a "no." Two remedies are available, and either would resolve this comment. The stronger one is to make the claimed decisions decidable: convert each "Move on when…" cell into a checkable condition (e.g., for Results: every number in the abstract and text traced to a specific table cell or analysis output; for Discussion: every causal verb audited against the design), state what happens when a gate fails, and add the worked example requested in Comment 2. The weaker but honest remedy is to reframe: retitle and redescribe the paper as a decision-*oriented* teaching framework and remove gate language the content does not support. What the paper cannot do is keep both the branding and the current level of specification.

**Relevant literature:** The genre does not require algorithmic determinism — Sukhera's JGME guidance (references 10, 11) asks narrative reviews for transparency and reasoning, not executability — but a manuscript that brands itself on decisions invites, and should meet, a higher standard of operational specification than one that brands itself on advice.

## Comment 2 — Whole manuscript; Figure 1 (p. 7 L.19–20)

There is no worked example anywhere in the paper. The framework is never applied to a real or realistic manuscript — no resident's draft is taken through THINK, STRUCTURE, WRITE, VERIFY, and PUBLISH to show what each stage produces, where the gates bite, or what a loop-back (p. 10 L.6–7) looks like in practice. For a paper whose stated audience is novices and the educators who mentor them, this is the single highest-value addition available in revision: a one-to-two-page worked vignette (a de-identified resident manuscript, or a composite) showing the one-sentence central message being drafted and rejected twice before it holds, the evidence skeleton discarding a display, a VERIFY audit catching a number that disagrees between abstract and table, and a revision action table filled in for two real reviewer comments. A worked example would also expose, better than any reviewer can, which of the framework's steps are rules and which are prompts — and would let the authors fix the soft ones before readers find them.

## Comment 3 — p. 6 L.4–19

The Methods describe the search at a level that cannot be reproduced or appraised. We are told the databases, the date ("through August 22, 2026"), the concept domains, and five prioritization criteria, but not the yield: no counts of records identified, screened, or included; no example search strings; no account of how the 19 references that survive into the paper were distributed across the five criteria or the five framework domains. The saturation claim — searching "continued until additional sources no longer changed the framework domains" — is unfalsifiable as reported, and it carries a circularity risk the paper should acknowledge: the domains that define saturation were defined by the same single author who ran the search, so "no new domains emerged" may mean only that the framework was fixed early. SANRA's third item (description of the literature search) asks for enough detail that a reader can judge the search's adequacy; a short table (source type, n retrieved, n included, role in the framework) would satisfy it without converting this into a systematic review, which neither SANRA nor I am asking for. As it stands, a 19-item corpus is a thin base for a synthesis claiming five domains, and the reader has no way to tell whether that thinness is the literature's or the search's.

## Comment 4 — p. 6 L.1–5; p. 7 L.1–5; p. 12 L.27–33

Measured against the SANRA scale the paper invokes (reference 12), the review is uneven. Justification of the topic's importance: adequate. Statement of aims: adequate (p. 5 L.19–21). Description of the literature search: partial, for the reasons in Comment 3. Referencing: partial — see Comment 5 and the Reference Audit; one load-bearing citation cannot be verified as printed, and several claims rest on sources further from the claim than the text implies (Comment 7). Scientific reasoning: adequate, and better than most framework papers — the distinction between authoritative guidance and "experiential or expert-derived" strategies is drawn explicitly and honored in most places. Presentation of endpoint data: partial — the framework is presented clearly, but with no worked example and no evaluation data, the "endpoints" of this review are asserted rather than shown. My item scores are 2, 2, 1, 1, 2, 1 (9/12). The paper does not need to become a systematic review; it needs its two 1-scored items (search description, referencing) brought up to the standard of its reasoning.

## Comment 5 — p. 8 L.1–2; p. 8 L.21–22; References, p. 14 L.3–4

Reference 6 cannot be verified as cited, and it is load-bearing. As printed — "Mensh B, Kording K. Finding your scientific story by writing backwards. Zool Res. 2023;44(2):371-376. doi:10.24272/j.issn.2095-8137.2022.311" — the DOI resolves nowhere (Crossref returns 404; doi.org returns 404), PubMed contains no Mensh article in *Zoological Research* and no record matching the citation, and the journal's 2023 volume contains no such article that I can locate. The entry appears to conflate two real sources: the title belongs to Montagnes DJS, Montagnes EI, Yang Z, "Finding your scientific story by writing backwards," *Marine Life Science & Technology* 2021;4:1-9 (doi:10.1007/s42995-021-00120-z), while the author pair's scientific-story paper is Mensh B, Kording K, "Ten simple rules for structuring papers," *PLoS Computational Biology* 2017;13(3):e1005619 (doi:10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619). This matters beyond one line of the reference list: reference 6 is the sole support for the THINK stage's core move ("building the narrative backward from the strongest defensible conclusion," p. 8 L.1–2) and is cited again for the WRITE drafting rationale (p. 8 L.21–22). It is also difficult to square with the disclosure that the author "independently verified cited sources and scientific claims" (p. 13 L.17–20) and with Box 1's instruction that "every important citation was opened and checked against the original source" (p. 12 L.2–3). I do not raise this as an allegation — the pattern is consistent with a drafting error, of exactly the kind the VERIFY stage exists to catch — but the correction is not optional: cite the source actually relied on (the Mensh & Kording paper is the canonical one for this argument and should probably be cited in its own right), and re-verify every remaining reference against its primary source before resubmission. My own audit (below) verified the other 18 references; the authors should not take my word for them either — that is rather the paper's point.

## Comment 6 — p. 10 L.8–11; p. 10 L.12–41 (Table 2); p. 11 L.1–3

The AI-governance synthesis omits the two policy sources most operative for this paper's readers. ICMJE (references 8, 9) and WAME (reference 15) are cited and — I verified — characterized accurately; the paraphrase of ICMJE §V.A at p. 10 L.8–11, including "incorrect, incomplete, biased," is faithful to the source. But the Committee on Publication Ethics' position statement, *Authorship and AI tools* (COPE Council; doi:10.24318/cCVRZBms), is the third member of the canonical policy triad — it is the source editors actually apply to the authorship and disclosure questions Table 2's RED zone governs ("deciding authorship," "concealing AI use") — and it appears nowhere. More surprising for a manuscript submitted to this journal: JGME's own 2026 editorial guidance on AI use and disclosure expectations for authors, reviewers, and editors (JGME 2026;18(3)) is never engaged, even though the PUBLISH stage instructs residents to align with journal policy and the paper elsewhere cites a JGME webpage for the journal's evaluation criteria (p. 10 L.45–47). A resident who follows this framework at JGME will be held to JGME's stated expectations; the framework should quote them. Both additions are short and would materially strengthen the paper's claim to currency — which is otherwise good: the January 2026 ICMJE update is captured, and few 2026 submissions manage that.

## Comment 7 — p. 8 L.8–17

The STRUCTURE stage instructs novices to identify "the" reporting guideline for their design and then names six — CONSORT, STROBE, PRISMA, STARD, CARE, TRIPOD (p. 8 L.11–12) — while citing none of them, only the EQUATOR hub (reference 7). A hub citation cannot do the work the stage requires: the novice's actual task is to open the statement and its checklist, and the current statements are neither named in the references nor linked. Cite the statements themselves (for CONSORT, the current version is the CONSORT 2025 statement, *BMJ* 2025; doi:10.1136/bmj-2024-081123). Two audience-fit problems follow. First, the first papers residents actually write in GME are disproportionately quality-improvement and education projects, for which the operative guideline is SQUIRE 2.0 — absent from the list, and its absence will be felt by exactly the readers the title promises. Second, the paper gives no guidance for the common case in which a design maps imperfectly onto any single guideline, which is where novices most need a decision and where the framework, again, offers a destination without a route.

## Comment 8 — p. 7 L.1–5; p. 12 L.22–26

The empirical support for the framework's educational claims is two studies, and both are further from the paper's claims than the text lets on. Reference 17 is a self-guided writing tutorial evaluated in first-year UK physiology undergraduates, with self-reported confidence and understanding as outcomes. Reference 18 is an AI-assisted, mentor-guided narrative-review writing task evaluated in third-year medical students, with a perception and satisfaction questionnaire. Both are real, both are characterized without distortion, and the paper does concede that the evidence is "largely experiential or expert-derived" (p. 7 L.1–5). But neither studies residents, neither studies manuscript production, and neither measures a writing-quality or publication outcome — while the Discussion's mentorship claims (p. 12 L.22–26: the shared vocabulary "can make supervision more efficient," can structure "workshops, longitudinal writing curricula, or peer-writing groups") are stated without any citation at all. Either temper those claims to what references 17 and 18 can carry, or do the work of engaging the scholarly-writing instruction literature in residents and fellows, which exists and would serve the argument better than undergraduate confidence data.

## Comment 9 — p. 10 L.12–41 (Table 2)

Table 2 is the paper's most operational element — and its boundary problem is therefore the most instructive. The zones are defined by example lists, and the examples do not cover the tasks residents actually bring to these tools, nor do they define a rule for classifying a new task. Is an AI-drafted Discussion paragraph built from the author's own bullet points GREEN ("organization," "alternative wording") or YELLOW ("interpretation prompts")? Is AI translation of the author's own completed manuscript the same risk class as AI translation of source material (both sit in YELLOW as "technical translation")? Is using a model to *check* a statistical analysis GREEN, YELLOW ("statistical explanations"), or part of VERIFY's analytic-consistency audit? A one-sentence classification principle would fix the table's usability without lengthening it: for example, classify by whether the output can enter the manuscript without independent verification (then it is at most GREEN only if it cannot alter scientific meaning), and by whether the task touches data, attribution, or judgment (then it is RED). As printed, two conscientious residents can classify the same task differently, which is the determinism problem of Comment 1 in miniature — but here, unlike elsewhere, a fix is cheap because the table's structure is already rule-shaped.

## Comment 10 — p. 10 L.42–48; p. 11 L.1–5

The PUBLISH stage illustrates the paper's pattern at its clearest: excellent logistics, unexamined decisions. The pre-submission confirmation list (p. 11 L.1–3) is genuinely checkable — article type, limits, statements, disclosure — and the revision action table format (concern → response → exact change → location, p. 11 L.3–5) is a concrete, teachable artifact; both deserve to survive revision verbatim. But the stage's actual decision — *which journal* — is handled by the instruction to "confirm journal fit" with no fit criteria offered (audience, article-type match, the journal's recent table of contents, realistic acceptance tier, turnaround), and the one place criteria are gestured at is the uncited-in-detail claim that "JGME itself evaluates manuscripts for relevance to graduate medical education, connection with existing literature and best practices, methodological rigor, and writing quality" (p. 10 L.45–47), supported only by a webpage reference (16) given without URL or section. If the journal's criteria are worth teaching — they are — cite them precisely enough that a resident can find and apply them. Credit where due: the characterization of reference 13's roadmap at p. 10 L.43–45 is accurate against that article's abstract, and this section is where the paper's synthesis is most reliable.

## Comment 11 — Title; p. 5 L.1–23; p. 8 L.18–26; p. 9 L.3–62

There is a scope friction between the title's promise and the framework's presuppositions. THINK begins from a completed study ("what the principal finding was," p. 7 L.21–23) and WRITE/Table 1 are built on IMRaD original research. But the modal first papers of residents — case reports and QI/education reports — are not IMRaD-shaped: CARE is named in the STRUCTURE list (p. 8 L.11–12) yet no stage tells a case-report author what to do differently, and Table 1 has no row that fits a case report's Discussion or a QI report's intervention description. The framework also starts after the two decisions that most determine whether a first paper exists at all — whether the project is publishable scholarship and whether the question is answerable with the data the resident actually has. Either narrow the stated scope (first *original-research* manuscript) in the title and abstract, or add the minimal branching the audience needs: one paragraph per stage on the case-report and QI variants. The current middle position — a universal title over an IMRaD-specific workflow — will mislead exactly the novices least able to notice.

## Comment 12 — p. 6 L.20–31; p. 15 L.15–27 (cover letter); p. 16 L.4–11 (title page)

The single-author voice needs reconciling. The manuscript is written throughout in the first-person plural ("we conducted," "we propose"), the reflexivity passage describes a single author, and the cover letter oscillates between "We believe" (p. 15 L.15) and "The author declares no conflicts of interest" (p. 15 L.23) within a page. Editorial "we" is defensible, but a paper that makes reflexivity a methodological feature should be precise about who is speaking. Relatedly, the reflexivity passage describes the author as a resident physician and medical educator (p. 6 L.20–31), while the title page lists a Faculty of Human Medicine affiliation (p. 16 L.5) — readers will notice the tension, and one clarifying line (current role and training status) would remove it. This is a small point, but in a manuscript whose currency is trustworthiness, small imprecisions about the author sit awkwardly next to a stage named VERIFY.

## Determinism Audit

Requested by the editor's reviewer instructions for this manuscript: does the "decision-based framework" supply operational decision rules, or narrative heuristics? I inventoried every stage and every point at which the paper claims a decision is made. A decision point counts as *operational* only if the paper supplies defined inputs, explicit criteria or thresholds, branching logic for the cases that arise, a stopping rule, or a worked example of its application.

| # | Decision point (location) | What the paper supplies | Operational rule? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | THINK: fix the central message (p. 7 L.21–23; p. 8 L.1–7) | A form (question + principal finding + why it matters) and a mentoring prompt ("do the data actually support that sentence?") | No — the test is a question the novice must answer by judgment; no criterion for "support," no worked example |
| 2 | STRUCTURE: select the reporting guideline (p. 8 L.9–12) | Six guideline names; a pointer to EQUATOR (ref 7) | No — no design-to-guideline mapping, no rule for ambiguous or mixed designs |
| 3 | STRUCTURE: build the evidence skeleton (p. 8 L.13–17) | A screening test: a display that answers no objective "may be secondary, supplementary, or unnecessary" | Partial — a test exists, but the three-way disposition has no criterion for choosing among its branches |
| 4 | WRITE: drafting sequence (p. 8 L.23–26) | A fixed order (Tables/Figures → Results → Methods → Discussion → Introduction → Abstract → Title) | Prescriptive, not decisional — an order is executable, but it decides nothing; the paper expressly disclaims its superiority |
| 5 | WRITE: section gates, Table 1 "Move on when…" (p. 9 L.3–62) | Five exit conditions, all qualitative ("displays and text agree"; "interpretation is proportional to design, effect size, uncertainty, and prior evidence") | No — gate language without gate criteria; no thresholds, no specified fail-path |
| 6 | VERIFY: four audits (p. 10 L.1–7) | A defined procedure (cross-check numbers across elements; compare methods to results; open the primary source; check against the guideline) plus a loop-back norm | Partial — the most executable element in the paper; still no acceptance threshold (what counts as a citation "supporting" a claim, or how many discrepancies fail the audit) |
| 7 | AI use: traffic-light zones, Table 2 (p. 10 L.12–41) | Categorical rules with example tasks and a required safeguard per zone | Partial — genuinely rule-like for enumerated tasks; no assignment rule for unlisted or boundary tasks (see Comment 9) |
| 8 | PUBLISH: journal selection (p. 10 L.42–48; p. 11 L.1–3) | An instruction to "confirm journal fit"; a checkable list of submission logistics | No for the decision (no fit criteria); yes for the surrounding checklist, which is logistics, not a decision |
| 9 | PUBLISH: revision responses (p. 11 L.3–5) | A format specification (concern → response → exact change → location) and a norm (explain non-adoption) | Operational as bookkeeping; the decisions inside it (adopt vs. rebut) remain judgment, appropriately so |
| 10 | Box 1: First-Submission Decision Check (p. 11 L.50–54; p. 12 L.1–9) | Seven binary self-attestation items | No — no external check, scoring, threshold, or consequence; the "decision" is the author's own say-so |

**Bottom line:** 0 of 10 decision points supply a complete operational rule; 3 are partially operational (#3, #6, #7, with #9 operational only as a format); 0 worked examples apply the framework end to end anywhere in the manuscript. The honest verdict is therefore two-sided. As a factual matter, the suspicion that this is "a framework without anything deterministic" is substantially confirmed: wherever the paper claims a decision, the criterion for making it is left to the reader's unaided judgment. As a critique, it needs one refinement: this genre does not promise determinism — narrative-review frameworks in JGME (references 10, 11) promise organized judgment, not algorithms, and the paper itself hedges accordingly ("teaching heuristic," p. 12 L.29; "not a prescriptive guideline," p. 7 L.4–5). The valid objection is narrower and stronger than "not deterministic": the paper *brands* itself on decisions — title, abstract, "decision architecture," gate language ("Move on when…"), a "Decision Check" — and branding creates the obligation. The gap is fixable in revision (worked example; checkable gate criteria; a classification rule for Table 2; journal-fit criteria), and Comment 1 specifies the fix. It is not a category error, and it is not fatal.

## Minor Comments

1. The five web references (7, 8, 9, 15, 16) are given without URLs. JGME style requires them for online sources, and for reference 16 the URL and section are the only way a reader can check the claim it supports.
2. The abstract calls this a "critical narrative review," but "critical" is never defined in the Methods, and Sukhera's typology (references 10, 11) does not use the term as a design label. Define it or drop it.
3. Several motivating claims in the Introduction (p. 5 L.1–23) — about the unevenness of writing instruction and what residents are "frequently expected" to produce — are uncited. They are plausible; a narrative review should still anchor them.
4. Box 1 (p. 11 L.50–54; p. 12 L.1–9) contains seven items for five stages (VERIFY and PUBLISH appear twice). Numbering the items to Figure 1's stage order would make the box usable as the checklist it wants to be.
5. Reference 13's article number is printed in the pages position, which matches the publisher's format for that journal but should be checked against JGME reference style.
6. The cover letter (p. 15) is consistent with the manuscript's disclosures — commendable — but repeats the "we"/"the author" oscillation noted in Comment 12.

---

## Suggested Editorial Disposition

**Major Revision.** The manuscript's contribution — reorganizing first-manuscript instruction around five stages with verification as a named stage — is real, the writing is clear, the hedging is honest, and the AI currency is better than most 2026 submissions. But a paper cannot be published under a "decision-based" title while supplying no decidable decisions and no worked example; and it cannot be published with a load-bearing reference that does not exist as cited, in a manuscript whose thesis is citation verification. A publishable revision needs: (1) one end-to-end worked example and checkable criteria wherever gate language is used, or an honest reframing of the title and claims; (2) correction of reference 6 and a documented re-verification of the full list; (3) engagement with COPE and with JGME's own AI guidance; (4) a search report sufficient to appraise against SANRA; (5) citations to the reporting-guideline statements the STRUCTURE stage names, including SQUIRE 2.0. All five are achievable in one revision cycle, and the paper that results would be worth JGME's pages.
