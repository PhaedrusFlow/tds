## Confidential Comments to the Editor

This is a worthwhile manuscript for the Milestones Supplement: a national, ACGME-led program evaluation with 1,306 survey completers and 2,540 analyzed open-ended comments is a data set no other group is positioned to collect, and the authors are commendably plain about its central weakness (an indeterminable response rate, findings framed throughout as hypothesis-generating). The reference list is accurate where I spot-checked it, which is not a small thing.

Three matters deserve the editor's attention alongside the author-facing comments. First, authorship and interest: three authors are ACGME staff, one of them the Director of Milestones Development, and the evaluation was recruited and framed by the ACGME itself. That positionality is also the paper's access and its authority, but the manuscript as provided carries no disclosure or conflict-of-interest statement, and for a supplement article evaluating the authors' own program the editor should require one rather than leave readers to infer interests from affiliations. Second, completeness of reporting: the survey instrument in the supplement is mostly closed-ended, and none of those results appear here; the editor may wish to ask why the closed-ended strand of this evaluation goes unreported. Third, the AI-assisted analysis (ChatGPT generated the initial thematic organization) is disclosed in the Methods, which is to the authors' credit; the reporting detail around it is thin enough that the analysis cannot currently be appraised or reproduced, and Comment 1 specifies what is needed. None of these is disqualifying. With the reporting completed, I would expect to support publication, and I am willing to review the revision.

## General Comments to Authors

The manuscript reports the open-ended component of a two-phase national program evaluation of Milestones 2.0: 45 stakeholder interviews informed a survey completed by 1,306 respondents, whose 791 facilitator comments and 1,749 barrier comments were thematically analyzed into 3 facilitator themes and 7 inhibitor themes. The findings will be recognizable to anyone who has sat on a CCC, and several are genuinely useful: the dominance of perceived misalignment with real-world competence, the demonstration that front-end assessment streamlining shifts burden to CCC aggregation rather than removing it, and the grading-distortion theme, which deserves wider attention than its frequency might suggest.

The paper's strengths are its scale, its verbatim data, its honest limitations section, and recommendations that follow from the findings rather than exceeding them. Its weaknesses are concentrated in reporting rather than in design: the analysis pipeline (particularly its AI stage) is described too thinly to appraise; the mixed-methods framing promises integration the manuscript does not deliver; theme frequencies are ranked in prose but never counted in print; the respondents themselves are never described; and one interpretive passage (the EPA comments) reads its own data ungenerously in a way the Discussion later contradicts. All of these are correctable without new data collection, and the comments below are written to be rebuilt against.

## Comment 1: manuscript p. 4, line 59 to p. 5, line 13 (Methods) | The AI stage of the thematic analysis is disclosed but not described in enough detail to appraise

The Methods state that "ChatGPT (OpenAI, GPT-5.6 Sol, accessed February 6, 2026) was used to generate an initial thematic organization of the open-ended survey data," that two authors "independently cross-checked the AI-generated themes against source data for accuracy," and that a third author served as tiebreaker. This disclosure is welcome and ahead of much current practice. It is not yet sufficient, in four respects:

1. **Process.** What was the model given (all 2,540 comments at once, or in batches; with what instructions or prompts)? Did it propose themes, assign comments to themes, or both? How many themes did it propose, and how did the final 10 emerge from that proposal set?
2. **Verification.** "Cross-checked against source data" could mean anything from independent recoding of every comment to a plausibility read of theme labels. Please state the unit and coverage of the check (every comment assignment, a sample, theme definitions only), and report agreement in some form, even simple percent agreement with a count of disagreements resolved by the tiebreaker.
3. **Data governance.** Open-ended responses from a survey whose supplement promises respondents anonymity and that "only aggregated summary results will be shared with the ACGME" were processed by a commercial external model. Please state what de-identification, if any, preceded that processing, and whether the institutional review covered it. Readers in 2026 will ask; the manuscript should answer before they do.
4. **Reporting standard.** A short worked example (one theme, from raw comments to final label, showing what the model proposed and what the humans changed) plus alignment with the SRQR reporting standard would resolve most of this comment in under a page. The relevant literature is listed at the end of this review.

## Comment 2: Abstract, p. 2, line 17; Methods, p. 4, lines 15-34 | The design is labeled explanatory sequential mixed methods, but this manuscript delivers one strand of it, and Phase 1 is invisible

Three related problems. First, the Phase 1 interviews (45 stakeholders, purposively sampled, recorded, transcribed, "thematically analyzed") are described in four sentences and their findings appear nowhere; readers are told only that they "informed" the survey. The foundation study of a two-phase design cannot be a black box: at minimum, report who was interviewed by role, how the analysis proceeded (analysts, approach, and a citation for the thematic analysis method, which is nowhere cited), and the principal Phase 1 findings that shaped the instrument. Second, the Phase 2 survey is, per the supplement, predominantly closed-ended (satisfaction and effectiveness ratings across competencies and domains), and none of those results are reported or cited as reported elsewhere. A qualitative analysis of open-ended responses is a legitimate paper, but it is not an explanatory sequential mixed-methods report, because nothing quantitative is being explained. Please either integrate or explicitly cross-reference the quantitative strand, or describe the design in this manuscript as what it is. Third, the Methods invoke a "principles-focused program evaluation approach" without citation; the framework is Patton's and should be credited (see Relevant Literature), not least because principles-focused evaluation makes specific claims about how findings like these should be used.

## Comment 3: Results, p. 5, lines 22-35 and p. 8, lines 14-24; Limitations, p. 15, lines 7-24 | Themes are ranked by frequency throughout, but no theme frequencies are reported, and the unit of analysis is undefined

The Results report exactly three numbers about the corpus: 2,567 initiations, 1,306 completions, and 791 versus 1,749 comments. Everything after that is ordinal prose: misalignment was "by far the most frequently represented theme," facilitator themes one and two "accounted for most facilitator-related responses," grading distortion and change fatigue were "less frequently identified." The Limitations then caution, correctly, that "comment frequencies should not be interpreted as prevalence estimates." The manuscript cannot have both: if frequencies carry the argument, report them; if they do not, remove the ranking. The straightforward fix is a per-theme count of comments (with the denominator stated) in Tables 1 and 2. Relatedly, define the corpus precisely: which survey items generated the 2,540 comments (the two Section 3 prompts only, or also the many "please elaborate" fields attached to closed items), whether one respondent could contribute multiple comments to one theme, and therefore whether counts describe comments or people. As printed, a reader cannot tell whether the dominant barrier theme represents 900 respondents or 90 prolific ones.

## Comment 4: Methods, p. 4, lines 37-55; Results, p. 5, lines 22-35 | The respondents are never described, although the instrument collected exactly the data needed to describe them

There is no table or paragraph characterizing the 1,306 completers by role, specialty, program type, or CCC involvement, nor any description of the subset who wrote open-ended comments. The supplement shows the survey collected all of these (Sections 1.1 to 1.8). With an unknowable response rate, describing who did respond is the reader's only basis for judging transferability, and it is the standard remedy the Limitations gesture toward without supplying. Please add a respondent characteristics table, state how respondents who began but did not complete the survey were handled (were their open-ended responses analyzed?), and, if the data permit, note any gross differences between open-ended responders and non-responders.

## Comment 5: Title page, p. 1, lines 20-30; Methods, p. 4, line 15 | Positionality and disclosure are absent for an accreditor's evaluation of its own program

The author list includes the ACGME's Director of Milestones Development and two further ACGME staff; the ACGME recruited the evaluation team and distributed the survey through its own channels to the programs it accredits. None of this invalidates the work, and insider evaluation has real advantages the authors could claim explicitly. But the manuscript as provided contains no conflict-of-interest or disclosure statement and no reflexivity note, and respondents were evaluating their accreditor's flagship framework in a survey distributed by that accreditor, a context relevant to interpreting both the facilitators (social desirability) and the barriers (which are, notably, directed at the framework rather than the accreditor). Please add: a disclosure statement covering author roles in Milestones development and governance; a short positionality paragraph; and the safeguards that did exist (university IRB determination, anonymity, aggregated reporting through UIC) promoted from the supplement's introduction into the main text, where readers will actually see them.

## Comment 6: Supplement, Section 3, item 6 (bundle p. 27); Results, pp. 5-12 | The instrument asked directly about equity, and no equity finding is reported

Section 3 of the survey asks: "Do the Milestones support equitable evaluation of all learners? If so, how or how not?" Equity of assessment is a live concern in the CBME literature and one of the few topics the evaluation probed by name. No equity theme, subtheme, or null finding appears in the Results. If responses were analyzed and nothing coherent emerged, that is itself a finding worth two sentences. If they were excluded, the exclusion and its reason belong in the Methods. Silence on a question the instrument asked reads as selective reporting.

## Comment 7: Results, p. 10, lines 26-32; Discussion, p. 14, lines 10-16 | The reading of the EPA comments is ungenerous, and the Discussion disagrees with it

The Results state that requests for EPAs "suggest a misunderstanding of the structure and function of EPAs versus competencies." The exemplar quotes in Table 2 do not obviously support a deficit reading: "Could Milestones be competency-based (i.e., EPAs)? An entrustability scale may be more meaningful to CCCs and the trainees themselves" and "Implementation of EPAs to feed into Milestone assessment" are coherent design proposals from respondents, and the Discussion itself later recommends that programs "consider how Milestones can be more deliberately harmonized with Entrustable Professional Activities," which is what the respondents were asking for. Either present evidence that the EPA comments reflected conceptual confusion (some may have; quote them) or reframe the passage: respondents were signaling that the assessment architecture, not their understanding, is what they experience as incomplete.

## Comment 8: Conclusions, p. 15, lines 49-58 | The Van Melle mapping is asserted in the final section and never demonstrated

The Conclusions state that the themes "closely mirror the core components framework described by Van Melle and colleagues," listing four components. The framework plays no role in the stated design or analysis (which is described as inductive), so the mirror claim arrives as an unargued post hoc assertion, and as listed it is incomplete: Van Melle's framework has five components, and the one the Conclusions omit, competency-focused instruction, is arguably the least visible in these data, which is interesting rather than incidental. A small mapping table (theme by component, including where the data are thin) would convert this from decoration into the paper's theoretical contribution; absent that, the claim should be tempered to partial correspondence. The verified citation is in the Relevant Literature below.

## Minor Comments

1. **Abstract, p. 2, lines 34-39 (Abstract vs Results, inhibitor count):** The Abstract lists inhibitors in a way that merges time burden and administrative burden ("implementation and administrative burden"), while the Results treat Time Burden and Administrative and Reporting Burden as two of seven distinct themes and argue explicitly that they are "related to, but distinct." Make the enumeration identical in Abstract, Results, and tables.
2. **Supplement, title page (bundle p. 23):** The supplemental instrument is headed "Milestones 3.0 Survey" throughout, for an evaluation of Milestones 2.0. If 3.0 names the project rather than the framework version, say so once; otherwise retitle, because as printed it invites confusion about what was evaluated.
3. **Tables 1 and 2 (bundle pp. 20-22), verbatim quotes:** Several exemplar quotes in the review copy appear garbled or truncated, running fragments together (Table 1, first row), and at least one contains an apparent transcription error ("where they resident is"). Please verify every table quote against source data and proof the tables independently of the text.
4. **Table 2 (bundle p. 22), Grading Distortion:** One quote describes strong residents being "graded down" to slow their progression, a deflation mechanism the text does not discuss; the prose treats distortion as inflation only. Address both directions.
5. **Introduction, p. 3, line 19 (historical framing):** "It would take 20 years" from the 1978 WHO monograph to the 1999 ACGME competencies is 21 years; trivial, but this readership will count.
6. **Supplement, Introduction (bundle p. 23); Results, Change Fatigue theme:** The supplement states that Milestones 2.0 development concluded in 2023; the survey fielded in 2024 to 2025 therefore measures remembered transition costs. One clause acknowledging the recall interval would strengthen the theme's interpretation.
7. **Cover letter (bundle p. 2) vs Title page, p. 1, line 43:** The corresponding author's mail code differs between the cover letter letterhead (MC 13-34) and the title page (MC 26-31). Please confirm the current one; correspondence errors in proofs are perennial.
8. **Cover letter (bundle p. 2) vs manuscript header, p. 1, line 5 (supplement naming):** The cover letter frames the target as the "Milestones Supplement" while the manuscript header reads "Milestones Supplemental." Harmonize to the supplement's official name.

## Suggested Editorial Disposition

**Major Revision.**

1. The contribution is real and, for this supplement, close to irreplaceable: nobody else can field a national sample on Milestones implementation at this scale, and the inhibitor findings (misalignment with clinical performance, burden displacement onto CCCs, grading distortion) are the kind of evidence the supplement exists to publish.
2. The deficiencies are reporting deficiencies, and every one is repairable from data the authors already hold: the AI analysis pipeline can be described and its verification quantified; theme counts exist by construction; respondent characteristics were collected; the quantitative strand can be integrated or cross-referenced; disclosure and positionality statements can be written in an afternoon. A rejection would punish fixable omissions; a minor revision would understate how much of the paper's credibility currently rests on unreported method.
3. Two comments are conditions of my support rather than suggestions: Comment 1 (the AI analysis must become appraisable, including the data-governance statement) and Comment 5 (disclosure and positionality for an accreditor-authored evaluation). The remaining major comments admit more than one acceptable remedy, and I have indicated the simplest in each case.
4. I would be glad to review the revision, and I would expect to move quickly: the checks are concrete (per-theme counts present, respondent table present, Phase 1 summarized, equity disposition stated, EPA passage reframed or evidenced, Van Melle mapping shown or tempered).
