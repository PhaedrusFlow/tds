7 Abstract
8
9 Background: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being explored in
10
11 academic medicine, but their reliability for substantive research evaluation remains
12
13
14 uncertain.
15
16
17 Objective: This study assessed the performance of three LLMs in evaluating pathology
18
19 research posters compared with a human faculty expert.
20
21
22 Methods: In this 2026 study, one pathology faculty member and three LLMs (ChatGPT-
23
24
25 5.2, Gemini-3, and Claude Sonnet-4.5) independently evaluated 90 de-identified posters
26
27 using a standardized rubric. The rubric included a modified poster type classification and
28
29
30 three ordinal domains: methods strength, conclusion validity, and writing quality, each
31
32 scored on a 1–5 scale. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Krippendorff’s α, Gwet’s
33
34
35
AC2, and weighted kappa.
36
37

39

41

44
45 from 23.1% to 57.8%. Weighted kappa values were low across domains, ranging from
46
47 0.058 to 0.226, with relatively higher agreement for writing quality. Agreement among
48
49
50 LLMs consistently exceeded agreement between LLMs and the human evaluator. Scoring
51
52 patterns also differed: ChatGPT assigned perfect scores across all three ordinal domains
53

55 to 12.22% of posters, compared with 5.56% for Claude, 4.44% for Gemini, and none by

4 Introduction
5
6
7
8
Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as influential tools in healthcare and
9
10 academic medicine, with the ability to process large volumes of text, generate summaries,
11
12 and support a range of language-based tasks that may reduce administrative burden and
13
14
15 assist scholarly work 1,2. Built on deep learning and natural language processing
16
17 architectures, LLMs have demonstrated strong performance in tasks such as
18
19
20 summarization, content generation, and contextual reasoning 1,3. Consequently, they are
21
22 being increasingly investigated for use in medical education, research support, and
23
24
25 academic writing processes, such as manuscript drafting, peer review facilitation, and
26
27 research assessment 3–5.
28
29
30 Even with their growing capabilities, LLMs are being adopted in academic medicine at a
31
32
33 measured pace, largely because of concerns about reliability, transparency, and
34
35 academic integrity. A key limitation is the tendency of LLMs to produce incorrect or
36
37
38
fabricated information, often referred to as “hallucinations” which can include inaccurate
39
references, misleading statements, or incorrect clinical reasoning 2,3,6. Additional
40
41
42 challenges include variability in output consistency, difficulty keeping up with the latest
43
44
45 literature, lack of access to subscription-only content and reduced accuracy in highly
46
47 specialized domains 6–8. Consequently, current evidence suggests that LLMs are best
48
49
50 positioned as assistive tools that augment human expertise rather than replace it 1,3,7.
51
52
53 Prior studies have evaluated LLM performance in academic and clinical tasks, including
54
55 standardized examinations, manuscript review, and guideline-based assessments 3, 7, 9.
56
57
58 While some studies report moderate agreement between LLMs and human evaluators in
59
60 structured settings, their reliability in more complex subspecialty areas and

31

41

 1
2
3
4 integration of LLMs into the evaluation process represents a promising avenue for
5
6
7 improving the efficiency and effectiveness of research assessments in medical education
8
9 7,11. In recent years, the excitement surrounding AI-assisted evaluation has surged, yet
10
11
12 a significant gap persists in our understanding of how well LLMs can mimic human
13
14 judgment in academic assessments. While many studies have explored the capabilities
15
16
17 of individual models or specific tasks, they often overlook the broader picture. There is a
18
19 lack of comprehensive research that systematically compares various LLMs against
20
21
human expert evaluations, particularly using structured rubrics and diverse reliability
22
23
24 metrics. This gap calls for a more profound investigation into the reliability of LLMs across
25
26 multiple evaluators and models in real-world academic settings, paving the way for future
27
28
29 studies to explore their potential in enhancing educational assessments 12,13.
30
31
32 Addressing a significant gap in the literature, this study utilizes a multi-modal, multi-rater
33
34
35 framework to evaluate the performance of three LLMs (ChatGPT-5.2, Gemini-3, and
36
37 Claude Sonnet-4.5) in assessing pathology research posters. By benchmarking these
38
39
40 models against a human faculty expert using a standardized rubric, the research
41
42 examines consistency across categorical and ordinal domains. A core contribution of this
43
44
work is its methodological rigor, featuring a systematic inter-rater reliability analysis of
45
46
47 human-AI and AI-AI pairs within an authentic academic environment. Ultimately, this
48
49 study aims to quantify the efficacy of LLMs in structured scholarly assessment, defining
50
51
52 their current utility and potential to enhance the evaluation of academic research.

4 Methods
5
6
7
8 Study Design and Context
9
10
11
12
This study employed a structured, multi-stage evaluation design conducted in 2026 to
13
14
15 assess inter-rater reliability between a human faculty expert and three large language
16
17 models (LLMs) in scoring de-identified student research posters (Figure 1). The four-
18
19
20 stage pipeline encompassed poster collection and de-identification, rubric development
21
22 and application, independent scoring by each rater, and statistical computation of
23
24
25 agreement indices across categorical and ordinal domains.
26
27
28
29 Sample and Evaluation Process
30
31
32
33 A total of 90 de-identified research posters were independently evaluated by one human
34
35 faculty rater and three LLMs (ChatGPT-5.2, Gemini-3, and Claude Sonnet-4.5), all
36
37
38 blinded to one another's assessments. Scoring utilized a standardized four-domain rubric:
39
40 poster type (categorical) and three ordinal quality domains (method strength, conclusion
41
42
validity, and quality of writing), each rated on a 5-point scale with no half-point increments
43
44
45 permitted (Supplemental Table 1-2). Poster type was assigned fixed values reflecting
46
47 study design complexity, with basic research receiving the highest score. Given the single
48
49
50 human rater, analyses focused on concordance between each LLM and this human
51
52 reference standard rather than traditional multi-human inter-rater reliability.

4 LLM Configuration and Prompting Protocol
5
6
7
8 All three LLMs were accessed via their respective web-based interfaces during February–
9
10
11
March 2026 under default system configurations, without API access or parameter
12
13 modification. Each model evaluated all posters independently in a single pass
14
15 (Supplemental Table 1-2) using a uniform, standardized prompt instructing the model to
16
17
18 adopt the role of an expert academic physician and score strictly according to the rubric,
19
20 without inferring beyond the provided content (Supplemental Table 1-2).
21
22
23 Outcome Measures and Statistical Analysis
24
25
26 Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize scoring distributions and perfect score
27
28 rates by rater. Agreement was quantified using four-way exact agreement and mean
29
30
31 pairwise exact agreement across all six rater dyads. Chance-corrected reliability was
32
33 assessed using Krippendorff’s α (nominal metric for poster type; ordinal distance
34
35
36 weighting for quality domains) and Gwet’s AC1/AC2 coefficients. Quadratic-weighted
37
38 Cohen’s κ was calculated for each rater pair to examine dyadic agreement patterns, with
39
40
benchmarks ranging from poor (< 0.00) to almost perfect (> 0.80) 14. A composite total
41
42
43 score (range: 6–20) was computed by summing all four domain scores, with the same
44
45 indices applied to this aggregate measure. Scoring stringency was characterized by
46
47
48 calculating perfect score frequency per evaluator, and convergent validity was assessed
49
50 by cross-tabulating each rater’s top three highest-scoring projects.

7 Descriptive Characteristics and Evaluation Efficiency
8
9 Ninety research posters were analyzed, with case reports comprising the majority
10
11
12 (78.9%), followed by case series (14.4%) and original research studies (6.7%). The
13
14 distribution across submission types was statistically significant (p < 0.001)
15
16
17 (Supplemental figure 1). Regarding evaluation efficiency, the faculty reviewer spent
18
19 approximately 5 minutes per poster (~7.5 hours total), whereas each LLM completed
20
21
evaluations in under one minute per poster (~1.5 hours total).

25 Agreement trends across raters and Domains
26
27
28
Poster-type classification achieved perfect agreement across all four raters, with four-way
29
30
31 exact agreement, mean pairwise exact agreement, Krippendorff's α, and Gwet's AC2 all
32

34
35
36 domains (Figure 2 and Supplemental table 3). For Methods Strength, four-way exact
37
38 agreement was 14.4% and mean pairwise agreement was 52.2%; Krippendorff's α
39
40

42

44
45

47

52

54

4 Discussion
5
6
7
8 This study evaluated the reliability of three LLMs (ChatGPT-5.2, Gemini 3, and Claude
9
10 Sonnet 4.5) relative to a human faculty rater in scoring 90 pathology research posters
11
12
13 across four domains: Poster Type, Methods Strength, Conclusion Validity, and Quality of
14
15 Writing. A striking divergence emerged between categorical and ordinal performance. All
16
17
18 raters achieved perfect agreement on Poster Type classification, confirming that LLMs
19
20 can reliably replicate expert categorical judgment when criteria are well-defined and
21
22
23
mutually exclusive consistent with prior literature11,15–18 and suggesting a role for these
24
25 models in early-stage screening and administrative review7,19, 20. Ordinal scoring
26
27 presented a contrasting perspective. Four-way exact agreement ranged from only 13.3%
28
29
30 to 24.4% across the quality domains, with weighted kappa values falling in the slight-to-
31
32 fair range for all rater pairs. The composite total score showed the highest chance-
33
34
35 corrected agreement (Krippendorff's α = 0.125; Gwet's AC2 = 0.702), a pattern partly
36
37 attributable to the stabilizing effect of the perfectly agreed-upon Poster Type
38
39
40 classification. A notable discrepancy between the two agreement indices was noted.
41
42 Krippendorff's α yielded very low values for ordinal domains (range: 0.013–0.062), while
43
44
Gwet's AC2 produced substantially higher estimates (0.601–0.644), reaching the
45
46
47 substantial range for Methods Strength (AC2 = 0.612). When scores cluster within a
48
49 narrow range, Krippendorff's α is susceptible to artificial deflation through its chance-
50
51
52 correction mechanism, a well-documented phenomenon known as the "prevalence
53
54 paradox" 21–23. Gwet's AC2 distributes its chance baseline more uniformly across
55
56
57 categories and is less prone to this distortion. Even so, AC2 values in this study generally
58

8

10 LLMs and the faculty reviewer differed not only in agreement levels but also in scoring
11
12
13 behavior. Pairwise weighted κ analysis revealed that AI–AI dyads consistently
14
15 demonstrated modestly higher concordance than human–AI dyads across all ordinal
16
17
18 domains. For example, ChatGPT and Gemini achieved a κw of 0.226, while the faculty
19
20 rater and Claude Sonnet achieved only 0.149 for Quality of Writing. This pattern suggests
21
22
23
that LLMs share latent scoring tendencies, likely arising from similar training paradigms,
24
25 that diverge from the implicit standards applied by experienced human reviewers. The
26
27 raters also differed meaningfully in scoring behavior. The human faculty applied the most
28
29
30 conservative ratings, awarding zero perfect composite scores, while ChatGPT assigned
31
32 perfect scores (5–5–5) in 12.22% of cases, Claude in 5.56%, and Gemini in 4.44%. The
33
34
35 faculty's conservatism likely reflects domain-specific expertise and implicit consideration
36
37 of factors beyond the rubric, including study feasibility, clinical relevance, and risk of bias.
38
39
40 This is illustrated by the Conclusion Validity domain: with 78.9% of posters being case
41
42 reports, a design inherently limited in capacity to change clinical practice, awarding a top
43
44
score is difficult to justify regardless of writing quality. LLMs, by contrast, appeared more
45
46
47 responsive to narrative clarity and writing quality than to methodological rigor, a tendency
48
49 consistent with findings by Latona et al., who reported that LLM-reviewed research is
50
51
52 more likely to be accepted than when evaluated by human experts 14. Collectively, these
53
54 findings reveal a significant "judgment gap" between LLMs and human expert reviewers.
55
56
57 Despite widespread variability, certain posters notably Projects 11, 65, 14, and 1 were
58
59 consistently ranked among the top contenders across evaluators. Project 11 achieved the

17
18 Despite current limitations, LLMs offer meaningful support in targeted applications. They
19
20 show promise in classifying research designs during early-stage review, reducing burden
21
22
23
on expert reviewers and freeing faculty time for mentorship and case-based learning 20,24.
24
25 All three LLMs also generated detailed evaluative comments without explicit prompting,
26
27 which could support scientific communication training and poster preparation 3,25,26.
28
29
30 However, increased reliance on LLM feedback risks reducing direct faculty contact,
31
32 important for developing skills in visual design, figure labeling, and scientific
33
34
35 argumentation. For high-stakes decisions such as summative assessment or grant
36
37 review, LLMs are not yet appropriate as independent evaluators. A hybrid model in which
38
39
40 LLMs assist rather than replace human reviewers reflects both the current evidence and
41
42 emerging consensus in the field 11. One practical approach involves calibrating models
43
44
using expert-provided scores and rationales prior to deployment to reduce systematic
45
46
47 scoring bias. LLMs currently lack the reliability needed for independent ordinal
48
49 assessment of research quality. Until calibration improves, high-stakes academic
50
51
52 evaluations should remain human-led, with LLMs serving as supportive tools that
53
54 augment rather than replace expert judgment.

 1
2
3
4 This study has several limitations. Only one human rater was used, preventing a robust
5
6
7 human interrater reliability benchmark. The sample came from a single pathology
8
9 educational meeting and consisted mainly of case reports, limiting generalizability.
10
11
12 Because posters include visual elements such as figures, tables, and microscopic
13
14 images, some LLMs may have been disadvantaged, particularly in pathology, where
15
16
17 image-based interpretation is central and LLM performance on image-based
18
19 assessments remains limited and in development 27. Multi-point rubrics also show limited
20
21 20,24,25.
reliability even among experienced human reviewers Finally, rapid LLM
22
23
24 development, opaque training processes, and rater agreement without proven validity
25
26 limit reproducibility and interpretation.
27
28
29
30 Future research should focus on improving rubric design, since rubric quality can
31
32 influence interrater reliability 20. Greater specificity or simplification may enhance LLM
33
34
35 scoring consistency; calibration exercises and hybrid human–LLM models could greatly
36
37 improve alignment with faculty judgment. Studies should also assess longitudinal score
38
39
40 stability, performance across disciplines, and the educational impact of AI-assisted
41
42 feedback. Methods such as chain-of-thought prompting may clarify why LLMs diverge
43
44
from experts, especially when judging conclusion validity. Overall, LLMs show promise
45
46
47 for classification and structured evaluation, but high-stakes academic assessment should
48
49 remain human-led, with LLMs serving as a complementary support tool.
