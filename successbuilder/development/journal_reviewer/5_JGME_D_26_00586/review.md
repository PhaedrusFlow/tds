# Comments to Authors

## Comment 1  
**Page 4, Lines 11-14**

The abstract’s Methods statement notes that data were “collected during the 2025 academic year,” whereas the main Methods section later specifies that all resident roster data were abstracted from program websites “between July of 2024 and December of 2024,” creating a basic but important inconsistency in the study timeline. Because the manuscript’s framing depends heavily on its position “five years post ACGME merger” and on the program inclusion criteria specifying “PGY1 Class of 2029 through PGY5 Class of 2025,” the exact observation window needs to be stated consistently across the abstract and body so readers can understand whether the analysis reflects a 2024 cross-sectional roster review, a 2025 academic-year snapshot, or some combination of the two. This section would therefore be stronger if the authors reconciled the abstract and methods language, explicitly defined the observation window, and clarified whether “2025 academic year” refers to resident class year labeling rather than calendar-year data collection.

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Applicants were grouped on matching in 2015-2019 (premerger) or 2020-2024 (postmerger).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).

## Comment 2  
**Page 5, Lines 1-10**

The introduction currently combines historical background, policy rationale, and benefit claims about the single accreditation system into one dense paragraph, and several phrases—for example, that the single system “streamlines graduate medical education,” “expands opportunities,” and “fosters greater collaboration”—read more like institutional advocacy language than neutral scientific framing. While these statements may accurately reflect ACGME’s stated goals, the post-merger literature suggests that outcomes for osteopathic applicants have remained mixed and specialty-specific rather than uniformly beneficial. This section would be more balanced if the authors explicitly attributed these benefit statements to ACGME or similar institutional sources and then contrasted those goals with empirical data showing that DO representation may improve in some settings while remaining constrained in others.

Supporting source passage: Ho et al., *Journal of Osteopathic Medicine* (2025): “In 2020, a merger occurred between the American Osteopathic Association (AOA) and the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), forming a single accreditation system (SAS) for graduate medical education and residency placement, with the purpose to create consistency within graduate medical education and to provide equal opportunities for applicants pursuing all specialties in medicine.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39843391/).

## Comment 3  
**Page 5, Lines 18-23**

The manuscript states that this study provides a “unique perspective” and is the “most recent assessment” of post-merger effects, but those claims are not yet well situated within the current literature. There is now a growing body of work examining DO versus MD match rates and representation in competitive specialties after the merger, so without directly comparing the present study to that literature, the novelty claim risks feeling overstated. This paragraph would be more persuasive if the authors specified what is actually distinctive about their design—such as the five-year post-merger time point, the explicit regional breakdown, or the use of contemporaneous PGY1-versus-PGY5 class composition as a proxy for recent differences in representation—and then explicitly contrasted that contribution with prior studies so readers can see what gap is being filled.

Supporting source passage: Garcia et al., *American Journal of Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation* (2025): “Using publicly available AAMC and NRMP data from 2012-2024, this retrospective study analyzed…” and concluded, “These trends suggest the merger influenced application and match dynamics but coincided with other shifts in medical education.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41248255/).

## Comment 4  
**Page 6, Lines 1-5**

The statement of study aim uses the language of “trends” and suggests that the manuscript is evaluating changes in DO representation five years after the merger, but the actual design appears to be cross-sectional, comparing PGY1 and PGY5 cohorts observed at a single time point via public program websites. That design can describe differences in current class composition across specialties and regions, but it cannot establish longitudinal trends with the same strength as studies that compare match outcomes across multiple years or explicitly track applicants over time in relation to pre- and post-merger periods. The wording here would be clearer and more methodologically aligned if the authors explicitly stated that they are using contemporaneous PGY1 and PGY5 representation as a proxy for recent differences in resident composition, rather than implying that the study directly measures temporal change attributable to the merger.

Supporting source passage: Wood and Krumrey, *Journal of Osteopathic Medicine* (2024): “This research aims to analyze the trends in orthopedic surgery match rates for DO vs MD applicants since the single accreditation merger…” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38511719/).

## Comment 5  
**Page 6, Lines 11-24**

The methods section explains that degree data were collected from public residency program websites and that programs were excluded when required information was not available, but it does not provide enough detail about how the authors handled incomplete rosters, ambiguous degree designations, outdated websites, or inconsistently formatted resident listings. This matters because program websites are highly variable in quality and update frequency, and studies that rely on website abstraction for residency demographics typically describe structured data-extraction rules and quality-control procedures so readers can judge the reliability and reproducibility of the dataset. The methods would therefore be substantially stronger if the authors explained how missing or uncertain data were handled, whether more than one reviewer abstracted information, how disagreements were resolved, and what criteria were used when degree type or PGY status was unclear on public listings.

Supporting source passage: Ames et al., *Journal of Orthopaedic Experience & Innovation* (2023): “The purpose of this study was to investigate differences in the demographic characteristics of orthopaedic surgery residents in 2021 in regard to sex, degree type, and race/ethnicity.” [Article](https://journaloei.scholasticahq.com/article/57307-the-demographic-make-up-of-orthopaedic-surgery-residents-in-the-united-states).

## Comment 6  
**Page 7, Lines 5-8**

The statistical methods section contains an apparent wording error, stating that DO representation was compared for “PGY5 and PGY5,” which almost certainly should read “PGY5 and PGY1.” More substantively, the choice to apply a paired t-test across nine AAMC regions for bounded percentage data that include zero values and likely small region-specialty cells is not adequately justified, especially given that the unit of analysis, pairing structure, and underlying assumptions are not fully described. This section would be more convincing if the authors corrected the PGY wording, explicitly defined the regional unit of analysis, reported raw counts alongside percentages for each region-specialty combination, and either provided a clear rationale for using paired t-tests or considered alternative methods more closely aligned with categorical proportion data.

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Statistical analysis using the Chi-Square test was performed to determine if there was a significant difference in the mean number of matches in the pre and postmerger groups (p < 0.05).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).

## Comment 7  
**Page 8, Lines 22-29**

The urology subsection contains a clear specialty-label error, stating that “DO urology residents account for 10% of all PGY5 orthopedic surgery residents,” and similar issues appear elsewhere, suggesting that some percentage descriptions may have been copied without fully updating the specialty labels. These may seem like minor copyediting issues, but in a results-heavy manuscript built around specialty-specific comparisons and regional percentages, repeated labeling errors can undermine readers’ confidence in the accuracy of the analysis and in the linkage between narrative statements and underlying tables and figures. This section would benefit from a careful line-by-line proofreading pass to verify that every specialty label, percentage, and narrative interpretation correctly matches the corresponding cells in Table 2 and the reported PGY1/PGY5 values.

Supporting source passage: DeFroda et al., *JBJS Open Access* (2023): “Among first-time applicants, MDs (93.1%) matched at a significantly higher rate than DOs/IMGs (68.6%, p < 0.0001).” [Article](https://journals.lww.com/jbjsoa/fulltext/2023/09000/a_comparison_of_match_rates_to_orthopaedic_surgery.9.aspx).

## Comment 8  
**Page 9, Lines 4-10**

The discussion opens by concluding that the findings “suggest limited benefits of the ACGME merger for osteopathic applicants” and “challenge the ACGME’s assertion that the merger enhanced opportunities,” but those claims extend beyond what the current cross-sectional design, based on program website rosters at a single time point, can directly support. This study evaluates resident composition in five surgical specialties using contemporaneous PGY1 and PGY5 class data; it does not directly examine applicant-level match rates, selection processes, pre-merger baselines, or causal mechanisms linking accreditation policy to individual opportunities. Published merger-impact studies generally use applicant-level or multi-year match data and still interpret their findings cautiously. The discussion would be stronger if it reframed these findings as descriptive evidence of ongoing heterogeneity in DO representation across specialties and regions, while acknowledging that broader causal claims about the merger itself require longitudinal match data and applicant-level analysis.

Supporting source passage: Garcia et al., *American Journal of Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation* (2025): “These trends suggest the merger influenced application and match dynamics but coincided with other shifts in medical education.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41248255/).

## Comment 9  
**Page 10, Lines 14-26**

The limitations section appropriately notes that only five surgical specialties were included and that some programs were excluded due to limited access to resident information, but it does not yet address several of the study’s most important methodological constraints. These include reliance on public program websites as the sole data source, potential issues with outdated or incomplete rosters, exclusion of programs with missing or unclear degree information, absence of applicant-level match data, instability of percentages in small regional cells, and the inability of cross-sectional PGY1/PGY5 comparisons to establish causal relationships or true longitudinal trends. The limitations paragraph would therefore be much stronger if it explicitly named these data-source and design constraints, acknowledged that large percentage changes may reflect very small denominators in some region-specialty cells, and clarified that the study describes current representation patterns rather than verified applicant opportunity or temporal trends attributable to the merger.

Supporting source passage: Jackson et al., *Impact of Program Characteristics on Diversity Representation in Orthopaedic Surgery Residency Websites* (2024): Paraphrase based on the PubMed abstract—this cross-sectional study of 198 orthopaedic surgery residency program websites found that program size and university affiliation were significantly associated with the number of diversity elements displayed on residency websites, underscoring that residency websites vary substantially in what they present. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39426316/).

## Comment 10  
**Page 17-18, Table 2**

Table 2 is central to the manuscript’s argument about regional representation, but it reports only percentages for MD and DO representation by region, specialty, and PGY level, without raw numerators and denominators, which makes it difficult for readers to assess how stable or meaningful many of the reported differences actually are. This concern is particularly salient in specialties such as plastic surgery and urology, where residency programs and resident counts per region may be small, so modest changes in one or two individuals could produce apparently large percentage differences between PGY1 and PGY5. Table 2 would therefore be much more informative if the authors added raw MD and DO counts for each region-specialty-PGY cell and, ideally, provided a supplemental appendix with program-level or regional totals.

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Postmerger, the proportion of osteopathic students matching into general surgery residency increased significantly (DO, 5.77% vs 14.25%, p = 0.032).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).
