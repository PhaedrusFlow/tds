## Confidential Comments to the Editor

This manuscript addresses a timely question for medical educators and includes several potentially useful elements, including 90 research posters, three contemporary LLMs, a shared scoring rubric, and multiple agreement coefficients. However, I recommend rejection in its current form because the principal limitations are structural rather than editorial. One human rater is treated as an expert reference standard, although the study cannot distinguish model-human disagreement from that individual's rater severity or error. Each model was run only once, so the study does not assess within-model repeatability despite making claims about reliability. The rubric has contradictory anchors and combines distinct constructs, while at least one inferential analysis appears to treat repeated ratings of the same posters as independent observations.

The technical execution is also insufficiently documented to establish that the models evaluated equivalent poster content under equivalent conditions. The manuscript does not adequately report file formats, rendering and OCR pipelines, model-specific document parsers, execution harnesses, repeated-run or retry loops, conversation state, tool availability, or other interface features that could influence model output. Addressing these concerns would require additional expert ratings, repeated LLM evaluations, redevelopment and validation of the rubric, standardized input processing, transparent execution logs, and substantial statistical reanalysis. These changes would constitute a materially redesigned study rather than a conventional revision. The underlying question remains worthwhile, and a future study with multiple independent faculty raters, adjudicated reference scores, repeated model runs, controlled document representations, and paired-data analyses could make a useful contribution.

## General Comments to Authors

This manuscript examines whether three large language models can classify and score 90 pathology research posters in agreement with a faculty evaluator. The topic is timely and relevant to educators considering AI-assisted evaluation of scholarly activity. The inclusion of multiple commercial models, de-identified posters, a common rubric, and several agreement measures are strengths. The finding that agreement is higher for a simple categorical task than for multidimensional quality ratings is also plausible and potentially useful.

However, the study in its current form cannot support its principal claims about LLM reliability or comparison with human experts. There is only one human rater, no human-human reliability benchmark, no adjudicated reference standard, and no repeated run of any LLM. The design therefore measures agreement among four individual score sets rather than reliability of the models or validity relative to expert judgment. The rubric introduces additional uncertainty because it contains contradictory scoring anchors, rewards study category as part of the total score, and combines methodological validity, importance, writing, and visual presentation within broad domains. Statistical reporting is incomplete, and the reported chi-square comparison appears inappropriate for paired observations because all four raters evaluated the same 90 posters.

These concerns are compounded by insufficient detail about the models' inputs and execution environments. A native PDF, rasterized poster image, OCR-derived text file, or platform-generated document representation may expose substantially different content to a model. Likewise, a manual web interaction, automated API call, agent harness, retry loop, tool-enabled session, or stateful conversation may produce meaningfully different behavior. Without standardization and reporting of these factors, the study may compare entire model-interface-document pipelines rather than the underlying evaluative abilities of the LLMs. Several interpretations in the Discussion also assume that the faculty score is correct, characterize differences as LLM score inflation, and attribute AI-AI agreement to shared training tendencies, although none of those explanations was tested. The manuscript would require a substantial redesign involving multiple expert raters, repeated model runs, a revised and piloted rubric, controlled inputs, reproducible execution details, appropriate paired analyses, and more cautious interpretation.

# Comments to Authors

## Comment 1
**Page 7, Lines 33-52**

The central comparison relies on one faculty rater, yet the title refers to "Human Experts" in the plural and the manuscript repeatedly treats the faculty scores as an expert reference standard. A single rater cannot establish expert consensus, quantify ordinary human-human variation, or show whether a disagreement reflects LLM error, faculty error, or a difference in rater severity. This concern is reinforced by the Discussion, which describes the faculty evaluator as conservative and suggests that the evaluator may have considered factors beyond the rubric. The study therefore compares three models with one person's scoring pattern rather than with a validated human benchmark. A stronger design would include at least two, and preferably three, independent pathology faculty raters; report human-human agreement; adjudicate discrepancies to establish reference scores; and compare each model with both individual and consensus ratings. The title, abstract, and conclusions should otherwise state clearly that the comparator was one faculty rater and that no claim about agreement with human experts as a group can be made. The authors should consider whether they would regard a single unadjudicated evaluator as an adequate reference standard when assessing a conventional medical diagnostic, educational, or research outcome. Prior medical-education work comparing faculty and AI scoring has used multiple faculty members to adjudicate discrepant scores rather than automatically treating one faculty score as correct [1].

## Comment 2
**Page 8, Lines 8-20; Page 15, Lines 12-19**

Each LLM evaluated each poster only once, which prevents assessment of within-model repeatability. Inter-rater agreement from one output per model is not equivalent to model reliability, particularly when the models were accessed through web interfaces with unspecified stochastic behavior. The design cannot determine whether ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude would assign the same score in a new conversation, on a second run, or after a minor change in the prompt. The authors should repeat each evaluation in independent, reset sessions; prespecify the number of runs and how repeated outputs will be summarized; and report within-model agreement before comparing models with one another or with faculty. Repeatability under unchanged conditions should also be distinguished from reproducibility after changes in model version, interface, input representation, or prompt wording [2].

The manuscript also does not explain how each model received or parsed the posters. Native PDF, PDF/A, PPTX, DOCX, SVG, TIFF, PNG, and JPEG inputs can preserve text, vector graphics, fonts, transparency, reading order, tables, legends, and microscopic images differently. Results may also depend on whether the authors used text-extraction packages such as PyMuPDF, pdfplumber, pypdf, Apache Tika, or Poppler; OCR systems such as Tesseract, PaddleOCR, EasyOCR, docTR, or OCRmyPDF; or rendering and conversion tools such as Poppler, Ghostscript, ImageMagick, GraphicsMagick, or LibreOffice. Package version, DPI, compression, color space, page scaling, orientation correction, table detection, multi-column reconstruction, and manual cleanup can all change what a model receives. Proprietary document parsers and vision pipelines built into the three web platforms may differ as well. The authors should report the original file type, every conversion step, package and version, settings, output format, resolution, reading-order method, failed parses, and manual corrections. Ideally, all models should receive the same controlled representations, with sensitivity analyses across native documents, standardized page images, and standardized extracted text. Otherwise, observed disagreement may reflect document ingestion rather than research-evaluation ability. Reporting guidance for LLM studies specifically identifies input data type and stochasticity management as essential elements [3].

## Comment 3
**Page 7, Lines 38-48; Supplemental Table 2**

The manuscript describes the rubric as standardized, but no evidence is provided that it was developed, piloted, or validated for this purpose, and the instrument itself contains major construct and scoring problems. In Supplemental Table 2, the Methods Strength domain appears to assign a score of 4 both when confounders are not adequately addressed and when methods are fully described, confounders are generally controlled, and statistics are appropriate. Conclusion Validity combines evidentiary support with novelty and likelihood of changing practice, although a conclusion can be valid without being practice-changing. Quality of Writing combines prose, organization, figures, and layout in a single score. Poster Type then assigns fixed points according to study design and adds those points to the composite total, systematically favoring basic research independent of the other quality domains.

These ambiguities could produce disagreement even if every rater applied the instrument conscientiously. The rubric should be corrected, separated into coherent constructs, reviewed by multiple content and assessment experts, piloted on an independent poster set, and accompanied by explicit anchors and examples for every score. Human raters should receive documented training and complete a calibration exercise before the definitive evaluation. The authors should also justify why study type belongs in a quality total rather than being analyzed as a descriptive classification variable. Research on scoring rubrics indicates that reliability depends on analytic criteria, task specificity, exemplars, and appropriate rater preparation [4].

## Comment 4
**Page 8, Lines 8-20; Page 9, Lines 4-9**

The model execution protocol is not reproducible enough to evaluate the reported comparison. The authors should identify the exact model name and snapshot, product tier, web interface or API, access date, geographic region if relevant, and any automatic model-routing behavior. They should report the complete system, developer, and user prompts; the order in which rubric and poster content were supplied; the output schema; and whether scores were manually transcribed or programmatically parsed. If an execution harness or agent framework was used, the manuscript should name its package, version or commit, source code, configuration, and control flow. Examples include custom Python or R scripts, vendor SDKs, LangChain, LlamaIndex, Semantic Kernel, AutoGen, or another orchestration layer. The authors should specify whether posters were processed sequentially or in batches, whether each poster began in a fresh session, whether item order was randomized, and whether caching or prior conversation state could carry information between posters.

The authors should also document any loops, retries, fallbacks, or automatic repair behavior. This includes the number of permitted attempts, retry triggers, timeouts, backoff rules, invalid-output handling, score-parsing corrections, fallback models, majority voting, self-consistency loops, reflection or critique passes, and whether the final response was selected from multiple candidates. Model settings should include temperature, top-p, maximum output tokens, random seed if supported, reasoning or extended-thinking mode, context-window use, and any truncation. The manuscript should state whether browsing, code execution, file search, retrieval-augmented generation, connectors, function or tool calling, computer-use functions, memory, citations, or other agentic features were enabled, disabled, or automatically invoked. A tool-enabled model may retrieve outside information, inspect a file through a separate parser, run code, or follow a multi-step harness, while another model may operate only on the visible prompt. Those are different experimental conditions.

For auditability, the authors should retain and, where ethically permissible, share de-identified request and response logs, timestamps, model identifiers, tool traces, errors, retries, token counts, and parsing outcomes. They should also explain how unpublished posters were authorized for upload to commercial services, which data-retention or model-training controls were selected, and how de-identification was verified. IRB exemption does not by itself resolve these data-governance issues. MI-CLEAR-LLM and TRIPOD-LLM emphasize reporting of model specification, access mode, inputs, prompt execution, stochasticity, evaluation settings, and human oversight [3,9].

## Comment 5
**Page 8, Lines 23-50; Page 10, Lines 28-58**

The statistical plan lists several agreement coefficients but does not identify a primary measure, report uncertainty, provide a sample-size justification, explain missing-data handling, or fully specify the analysis software. These omissions are consequential because Krippendorff's alpha and Gwet's AC2 yield sharply different interpretations for the same ordinal domains: alpha values are approximately 0.01-0.06, whereas AC2 values are approximately 0.60-0.64. The Discussion offers the prevalence paradox as a possible explanation, but the manuscript does not present the complete score marginals, weight matrices, or confidence intervals needed to evaluate that explanation, and it moves between coefficients in a way that can appear selective.

The authors should prespecify the principal agreement statistic, report 95% confidence intervals for every overall and pairwise coefficient, provide the exact weighting scheme, and show the marginal score distributions. They should identify the programming language, environment, package versions, and functions used - for example, R packages such as irrCAC, irr, psych, or krippendorff, or Python packages such as statsmodels, scikit-learn, pingouin, or a custom implementation - because defaults and weight definitions may differ. Analysis code and de-identified rating-level data should be made available if permitted. The composite score should also be reconsidered because it adds a fixed study-design score to three ordinal quality scores, producing a total whose construct and weighting have not been justified. Selection and interpretation of inter-rater reliability statistics should be matched explicitly to the measurement scale and study design [5].

## Comment 6
**Page 11, Lines 19-28; Figure 4**

The reported comparison of perfect-score frequencies appears statistically inappropriate or, at minimum, inadequately described. All four raters scored the same 90 posters, so their observations are paired rather than independent; a conventional Pearson chi-square test with Cramer's V would violate the independence assumption. If the outcome is whether each rater awarded a 5-5-5 score, a method for matched binary outcomes across more than two raters, such as Cochran's Q with appropriate post hoc comparisons, would be more defensible. If the authors intend to compare the original ordinal scores or composite totals, a repeated-measures ordinal method such as the Friedman test or an appropriate mixed-effects model should be considered. The analysis should also account for multiplicity if pairwise post hoc tests are performed.

This section calls 5-5-5 a "maximum composite score," although the Methods define the composite total as the sum of four domains with a range of 6-20. A 5-5-5 result is instead a ceiling score across the three ordinal quality domains. The test, effect size, figure annotation, and terminology should be corrected after statistical review. The Friedman test is specifically intended for ordinal observations obtained from the same units under repeated conditions [6].

## Comment 7
**Page 10, Lines 9-21; Page 17, Lines 43-57; Supplemental Figure 1**

The poster-type distribution is described as "statistically significant," but the manuscript does not state the null distribution being tested or explain why equal numbers of case reports, case series, and original studies would be expected. This hypothesis test does not appear to answer the study question and may add an unwarranted sense of inferential importance to a sample characteristic. Reporting is also inconsistent: the Results state p < 0.001, the supplemental caption states p < 0.05, and Supplemental Figure 1 displays only p < 0.05. The authors should either justify the prespecified null hypothesis and report one exact result consistently or remove the test and present counts and percentages descriptively.

The same paragraph compares faculty and LLM evaluation time without defining a timing protocol. It is unclear whether timing included file conversion, upload, OCR, prompt assembly, model queue time, tool calls, retries, output validation, score extraction, error correction, and human data entry. A harness that automatically loops through posters or performs concurrent requests is not comparable with a faculty member manually opening and scoring one poster at a time. The authors should define the unit of timing, concurrency, hardware or network conditions where relevant, excluded failures, and whether timing was prospectively logged. Without equivalent workflows and uncertainty across repeated observations, the efficiency claim should remain descriptive. Descriptive and inferential statistics should be aligned with a clearly defined population question rather than applied to sample counts without a meaningful comparator [7].

## Comment 8
**Page 12, Lines 18-25; Page 13, Lines 23-54**

Several interpretations go beyond the data. Perfect agreement on Poster Type demonstrates agreement in this sample, but it does not confirm that LLMs "reliably replicate expert categorical judgment" because there is only one expert comparator, no independent ground truth, only six original research posters, and no repeated LLM runs. Higher AI-AI than faculty-AI agreement likewise does not establish shared "latent scoring tendencies" arising from similar training paradigms. It could reflect one stringent faculty rater, rubric ambiguity, common response anchoring, similar document-parsing failures, shared tool access, or other unmeasured interface and harness effects.

Describing the LLM scores as inflation assumes that the faculty scores are correct. The statement that faculty conservatism likely reflects consideration of factors beyond the rubric is especially problematic because all raters were supposed to apply the same standardized criteria; if the faculty used additional implicit criteria, the comparison was not standardized. These passages should distinguish observed score differences from untested explanations and avoid treating agreement as accuracy or validity. Kappa quantifies agreement beyond chance but does not identify which rater is correct or establish validity of the rated construct [8]. The citation at Page 13, Lines 47-52 also requires correction: reference 14 is Landis and Koch, not the stated study by Latona et al.

## Comment 9
**Page 8, Lines 48-50; Page 11, Lines 28-38; Page 13, Lines 57-62 and Page 14, Lines 4-14**

The top-three project analysis is labeled "convergent validity," but overlap among raters is another form of agreement and does not provide independent validity evidence. The manuscript does not explain how ties were handled, whether the top-three procedure was prespecified, why only three projects were selected, or whether the event-day selections were made by the same faculty rater whose scores are already included in the analysis. The statement that Project 11 was the "only unanimously top-ranked project across faculty, Claude, and ChatGPT" is also misleading because Gemini did not select it and unanimity would ordinarily refer to all four raters.

The reported overlaps account for selected pairs but do not summarize concordance across the complete ranked dataset. This analysis should be relabeled as exploratory top-rank overlap, with tie handling and selection rules reported. If the authors wish to assess ranking agreement, they should consider a full-ranking statistic such as Kendall's coefficient of concordance or an appropriate rank correlation, with uncertainty. If they wish to assess validity, they need an external criterion independent of the raters being compared. Reliability, reproducibility, validity, and bias are distinct properties and should not be inferred from one overlap analysis [1].

## Comment 10
**Page 15, Lines 4-26**

The limitations section acknowledges one human rater and a single-site, case-report-heavy sample, but it does not fully state how those constraints affect the conclusions. It should explicitly acknowledge that the study did not measure human-human agreement, within-model repeatability, or accuracy against an adjudicated standard; that the rubric has not been shown to be valid; that score distributions and poster types are highly imbalanced; that only six posters were original research; and that timing was not evaluated under a controlled protocol. Generalizability to GME is also uncertain because the manuscript alternates between describing the materials as student posters and student/resident posters without reporting learner level, training program, selection criteria, meeting context, or the faculty rater's qualifications and experience.

The limitations should further acknowledge that commercial model updates, proprietary parsers, file conversion, OCR errors, image resolution, interface-specific safety filters, context truncation, conversation state, agent harnesses, loop and retry logic, tool calling, browsing, code execution, retrieval, structured-output enforcement, and automatic model routing may all limit reproducibility or confound attribution to the base LLM. The model versions should also be reconciled: the study repeatedly names Claude Sonnet 4.5, whereas Page 1, Lines 55-59 disclose Claude Sonnet 4.6 for manuscript assistance. Although these may represent separate uses, the distinction should be explicit. The title and conclusion should ultimately align with what was studied: a single-pass, single-human-rater comparison using one local poster sample, an unvalidated rubric, and incompletely described commercial model pipelines.
