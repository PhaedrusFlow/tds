## General Comments to Authors

This manuscript addresses an important question for dermatology applicants, advisors, and residency selection committees: whether taking additional time during medical school, particularly for research, is associated with sustained academic productivity after residency training. The long follow-up horizon is a meaningful strength, and the effort to examine outcomes beyond the immediate residency match is valuable because much of the literature on dedicated research years focuses on short-term application outcomes rather than longer-term academic careers. The manuscript also raises a relevant equity concern regarding the financial and temporal burden of an additional year of training. However, the principal methodological concern is that the exposure described throughout the manuscript as a “research gap year” does not appear to be directly measured as a research gap year. Participants are instead stratified by time spent in medical school (4 years, 5-6 years, and 7+ years), while the Methods separately note collection of additional degrees. Additional time in medical school may represent a dedicated research year, dual-degree training, leave, remediation, personal circumstances, or other pathways. Unless the authors can verify the reason for the additional year(s), the title, objective, interpretation, and conclusions risk attributing findings to research-gap-year participation when the analysis is actually based on medical-school duration.

Several additional issues limit confidence in the current interpretation. The cohort is drawn from a single dermatology residency program and therefore represents individuals who already successfully matched at NYU, substantially limiting generalizability to applicants or dermatology residents nationally. Publication and academic-appointment outcomes are reconstructed from public sources, but the manuscript does not provide enough detail about identity matching, name changes, incomplete profiles, missing data, or abstraction quality control. Follow-up begins after residency or fellowship depending on the participant, potentially introducing heterogeneity in the index date, and individuals who have not yet reached 10 years appear to be categorized as “missing” in Table 1 rather than administratively ineligible for that endpoint. The statistical description also requires clarification because the manuscript refers to “paired” analyses despite apparently independent groups and describes nonparametric tests while Table 2 reports mean differences and confidence intervals with a Scheffe adjustment. Most importantly, the manuscript’s own calculation indicates that 64 participants per group would be needed to detect a difference of 1 paper per year, whereas the primary groups contain only 45 and 31 participants. Failure to obtain statistical significance therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that research-year participation has no association with later productivity. The manuscript would be stronger if reframed as an exploratory single-program cohort study, with clearer exposure and outcome definitions, more transparent statistical methods, greater emphasis on effect estimates and confidence intervals, and conclusions limited to what this sample can support.

# Comments to Authors

## Comment 1 — Define the Exposure Precisely

**Manuscript Page 2, Lines 19-38; Manuscript Page 4, Lines 51-58; Manuscript Page 5, Lines 12-16**

The manuscript repeatedly refers to a “research gap year,” but the analytic exposure is duration of medical school (4 years, 5-6 years, and 7+ years). These are not necessarily equivalent. A fifth or sixth year may reflect a dedicated research year, an additional degree, a leave of absence, remediation, or another circumstance. Because the central question of the manuscript is specifically about the long-term value of a research gap year, the authors should explain how the reason for additional time in medical school was determined and report how many participants in each duration group actually completed a dedicated research year.

If research-gap-year status can be verified from ERAS applications or another source, the primary analysis should be based on that verified exposure. If it cannot be verified, the manuscript should be reframed throughout as a study of medical-school duration rather than research-gap-year participation. This issue affects the title, objective, abstract, Results interpretation, and Conclusions.

## Comment 2 — Narrow the Scope and Generalizability of the Claims

**Manuscript Page 4, Lines 14-43; Manuscript Page 7, Lines 4-19; Manuscript Page 8, Lines 14-19**

The study population consists entirely of NYU dermatology residency alumni, meaning that every participant had already successfully matched into one highly competitive program. This is a highly selected cohort and cannot establish the value of a research year for obtaining a dermatology residency position or predict outcomes among dermatology applicants more broadly.

The manuscript would be more methodologically aligned if the aim and Discussion explicitly described the study as an examination of long-term academic outcomes among alumni of a single dermatology residency program. Similarly, statements regarding the “career-scale value” of the gap year and recommendations to admissions committees should be tempered. A more defensible conclusion would be that no statistically significant differences were detected in this cohort, rather than that research years do not contribute to future academic productivity.

## Comment 3 — Strengthen Outcome Ascertainment and Reproducibility

**Manuscript Page 5, Lines 4-16**

Academic productivity and appointments were identified using PubMed, Doximity, LinkedIn, and professional webpages, but the manuscript does not provide enough information to assess the reliability or reproducibility of this process. Please describe how alumni were matched to publications when names were common or changed, whether middle initials, institutional affiliations, ORCID identifiers, or other identifiers were used, and whether publications outside PubMed were considered.

The definition of an “academic appointment” should also be operationalized. For example, did this require a faculty title at an academic medical center, and how were adjunct, volunteer, affiliate, or nonclinical appointments handled? Please also clarify whether more than one reviewer abstracted these data, whether abstraction was performed independently, and how uncertain cases or disagreements were resolved.

## Comment 4 — Clarify Follow-up, Fellowship, and Missing Data

**Manuscript Page 5, Lines 19-33; Table 1**

The manuscript defines the beginning of post-training follow-up as after completion of residency or fellowship. This creates different index dates for participants depending on whether they pursued fellowship, and fellowship participation may itself be associated with later academic productivity. Please report fellowship frequency by exposure group and explain the rationale for beginning follow-up after fellowship for some participants but after residency for others. A sensitivity analysis using a common index date, or adjustment for fellowship participation, would strengthen the analysis.

The handling of the 10-year academic-appointment endpoint also needs revision. The Methods state that participants who had not yet reached 10 years were considered “not applicable,” while Table 1 lists 19 of 45 participants in the 4-year group and 15 of 31 in the 5-6-year group as “Missing.” Participants who are not yet eligible for 10-year follow-up should be distinguished from genuinely missing outcome data, and percentages should be calculated using the appropriate eligible denominator.

## Comment 5 — Reconcile the Statistical Methods With Tables 1 and 2

**Manuscript Page 5, Lines 38-53; Tables 1 and 2**

The statistical analysis is difficult to reconcile with the reported tables. The medical-school-duration groups appear to be independent, yet the manuscript refers to a “paired analysis.” If the intended meaning is *pairwise comparisons*, that terminology should be used. The Methods also state that Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used, whereas Table 2 reports differences in means, lower and upper confidence limits, and Scheffe-adjusted P values. Please specify the exact statistical model used to generate the differences and confidence intervals in Table 2, explain how the multiple-comparison adjustment was applied, and state why these methods were chosen for highly skewed publication-count outcomes.

Table 2 also appears to contain at least one value that should be verified. For the 5-6-years minus 4-years comparison for papers per year during years 0-10, the reported difference is 0.062, the lower confidence limit is also 0.062, the upper confidence limit is 1.026, and the P value is 0.988. A confidence interval entirely above zero would not ordinarily correspond to a two-sided P value near 1.0, suggesting a possible transcription or calculation error.

## Comment 6 — Interpret the Null Findings in Light of Limited Power

**Manuscript Page 6, Lines 4-26; Manuscript Page 7, Lines 46-58; Manuscript Page 8, Lines 14-19**

The manuscript reports no statistically significant differences between the 4-year and 5-6-year groups and then states that 64 participants per group would be required to detect a difference of 1 paper per year with 80% power under the stated assumptions. The actual principal groups contain only 45 and 31 participants. This is important evidence that the study may not have sufficient precision to exclude an educationally meaningful difference.

The Results and Conclusions should therefore distinguish “no statistically significant difference was detected” from “there is no difference.” Please emphasize effect estimates and confidence intervals and explicitly acknowledge that meaningful differences may remain compatible with the observed data. The statement that completing a research year “does not portend increased academic productivity” is too definitive for an underpowered single-program analysis.

## Comment 7 — Characterize the 7+ Year Group and Multiple Comparisons

**Manuscript Page 6, Lines 31-48**

The 7+ year group is included in multiple pairwise comparisons and produces the only statistically significant result described in the Results, yet this group is not adequately characterized. Please report the number of participants in this group and explain why they spent 7 or more years in medical school. This group may disproportionately include MD/PhD trainees or individuals with other advanced degrees, which would make direct interpretation as a “gap year” group problematic.

The manuscript also evaluates multiple outcomes, time windows, authorship categories, and pairwise group comparisons. Please explicitly define which family of comparisons received the Scheffe adjustment and clarify whether the multiplicity correction was prespecified. Given the number of comparisons performed, the isolated statistically significant finding should be interpreted cautiously.

## Comment 8

**Manuscript Page 7, Lines 24-41**

The discussion of financial burden and equity is important and increases the relevance of the manuscript to advisors and selection committees. However, the present study does not directly measure socioeconomic status, debt, financial burden, access to paid research positions, or the source of funding during an additional medical-school year. These points should therefore be framed as implications supported by prior literature rather than as findings from the current cohort.

The equity argument would also be strengthened by resolving the exposure-definition issue. If the manuscript is intended to inform whether selection committees should value a dedicated research year, it is essential to distinguish a voluntary research year from other reasons a student may require more than 4 years to complete medical school.

## Comment 9 — Correct Source, Terminology, and Reference Inconsistencies

**Manuscript Page 3, Lines 12-19; Manuscript Page 9, Lines 7-43**

Several editorial and reference details should be corrected. The Introduction attributes match statistics to the 2024 match cycle, whereas Reference 1 is listed as the 2026 NRMP Advance Data Tables. Please reconcile the year and source. The phrase “30 of 101 senior applications” appears to mean “30 of 101 senior applicants,” and the DO match rate is reported as “29.7” without a percent sign.

The references should also receive a careful accuracy and formatting review. In particular, Reference 3 lists the page range as “75-576,” which appears unusual and should be verified against the source. Journal titles, author punctuation, capitalization, page ranges, and citation formatting should be standardized according to JGME requirements.
