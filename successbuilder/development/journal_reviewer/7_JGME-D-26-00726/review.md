## Confidential Comments to the Editor

The topic is timely, but I do not believe the manuscript meets the novelty threshold for publication as a JGME Perspective. The central contribution is a mapping of DEFT-AI back onto the One-Minute Preceptor (OMP), even though the original DEFT framework was itself explicitly developed as an enhancement of the OMP, and the 2025 *New England Journal of Medicine* review by Abdulnour, Gin, and Boscardin already adapted this framework for direct supervision of learner AI use. More importantly for editorial priority, **JGME itself published “Supervising Resident AI Use Without Losing the Learning” in February 2026**, which already presents DEFT-AI as a rapid bedside supervision tool, includes a “DEFT-AI in Under 2 Minutes” box, and supplies concrete prompts for identifying AI use, interrogating inputs/evidence, separating learner reasoning from AI output, verifying recommendations, and directing future AI use. The present submission therefore appears to repackage a framework and workflow that this journal has already published in more operational form rather than supplying a distinct educational model, implementation study, outcome evaluation, or materially new conceptual advance.

The redundancy is more concerning because both authors’ own institution—and both authors individually—already have substantially more informative work that is absent from the manuscript. Geringer is a coauthor of the 2026 *Military Medicine* study of AI use among military GME trainees, which found that 84% wanted formal AI training, 73% felt their current curriculum was insufficient, and attending role-modeling of AI use was low. Hemmer is a coauthor of the current 2026 JMIR preprint *How Medical Students Use Artificial Intelligence for Learning*, which reports AI use in 91.6% of surveyed first-year students and identifies failure to verify AI-generated information as a measurable curricular target. Hemmer was also a coauthor of the earlier JMIR preprint and subsequent 2024 publication evaluating ChatGPT for scoring medical-student clinical notes. These institution-specific data could have supported an original needs assessment, intervention, implementation study, or faculty-development evaluation. Instead, the submitted Perspective makes untested claims that the proposed integration is efficient and imposes little additional cognitive or time burden. I therefore recommend rejection rather than major revision; a publishable successor would need a clearly differentiated contribution, preferably prospective implementation and outcome data rather than another conceptual remapping.

## General Comments to Authors

The manuscript addresses an important problem: learners are already using AI in clinical work, supervisors need practical methods for making AI-mediated reasoning visible, and educational programs need ways to protect independent clinical reasoning while teaching appropriate AI use. I agree that brief, familiar supervisory structures are attractive in time-constrained settings. The difficulty is not the importance of the problem but the manuscript’s incremental contribution. The current paper largely maps an AI-adapted framework onto the educational framework from which it was already derived, while the cited literature—and JGME’s own 2026 publications—already provides closely overlapping prompts and supervisory guidance.

For a Perspective to justify publication, the manuscript needs to make clearer what readers can do, know, or test after reading it that they could not obtain from the existing DEFT, DEFT-AI, OMP, and JGME AI-supervision literature. In its current form, the paper does not provide implementation data, educational outcomes, faculty or learner acceptability, feasibility measurements, time-motion data, reliability evidence, a worked case demonstrating a unique decision pathway, or a substantively new theoretical construct. Several claims of efficiency and generalizability are therefore stronger than the evidence presented. I would encourage the authors to build on their institution’s own emerging empirical literature and test the framework prospectively rather than publish an additional conceptual mapping.

## Comment 1 — manuscript p. 3 | lines 7–19 and 24–39

The manuscript’s central novelty claim is difficult to sustain because the conceptual pathway is nearly circular. Savaria et al. (2022), which the authors cite as reference 6, explicitly described DEFT as an enhancement of the One-Minute Preceptor; Abdulnour, Gin, and Boscardin then developed DEFT-AI for supervision of learner AI use in *NEJM* in 2025. The present manuscript proposes to align DEFT-AI back with the OMP and states that it “expand[s] on prior work integrating DEFT-AI with the OMP,” but the cited reference at this point is Savaria’s pre-AI DEFT paper rather than the actual DEFT-AI source. Please identify the genuinely new construct or function produced by this remapping and correct the attribution. If the contribution is simply that DEFT-AI retains recognizable OMP microskills, that relationship appears inherent in the framework’s lineage rather than a new educational model.

**Relevant literature:** Savaria MC, et al. *Int J Infect Dis.* 2022;115:149-153. doi:10.1016/j.ijid.2021.11.046. Abdulnour REE, Gin B, Boscardin CK. *N Engl J Med.* 2025;393:786-797. doi:10.1056/NEJMra2503232.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40834302/

## Comment 2 — manuscript p. 3 | lines 26–31; manuscript p. 6 | lines 4–19

The manuscript repeatedly characterizes the proposed approach as feasible in busy workflows and states that it can be used “without adding substantial time or cognitive burden” and “without substantially increasing time burden,” but no implementation, timing, workload, acceptability, or cognitive-load data are presented. This is particularly important because JGME already published a more operational version of DEFT-AI in February 2026: *Supervising Resident AI Use Without Losing the Learning* includes a “DEFT-AI in Under 2 Minutes for Clinical Supervisors” box with concrete prompts covering disclosure of AI use, inputs, independent reasoning, verification, and future-use guidance. The authors should either provide empirical evidence that their OMP alignment improves speed, feasibility, retention, or educational effectiveness beyond that already-published workflow, or substantially soften these claims. Without such evidence, the manuscript does not establish that its remapping adds practical value beyond existing JGME guidance.

**Relevant literature:** Preiksaitis C. *J Grad Med Educ.* 2026;18(1):12-15. doi:10.4300/JGME-D-25-01133.1. PMID:41694785.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41694785/
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12903258/

## Comment 3 — manuscript p. 2 | lines 36–53; manuscript p. 5 | lines 14–26

The paper frames widespread unsupervised AI use and limited faculty guidance as the motivating gap, but it omits unusually relevant empirical evidence generated by the authors’ own institution and, for the first author, by the first author himself. Geringer coauthored *Artificial Intelligence in Military Graduate Medical Education: Trainee Perceptions and Current Use* (2026), a SAUSHEC/Uniformed Services University study in which 84% of respondents wanted formal AI training, 73% reported insufficient AI content in their curriculum, trainees reported current AI use for diagnosis, treatment, research, and teaching, and attending role-modeling was only 11%–22% depending on task. Those findings directly substantiate the manuscript’s stated implementation problem and are more specific to the authors’ actual GME context than several general citations currently used. The omission weakens the evidentiary basis and makes the Perspective appear detached from the authors’ own empirical program of work. Please integrate these data and explain how the proposed framework responds to needs identified in that study.

**Relevant literature:** Yabes JM Jr, Lindholm DA, Ford MB, Kiley J, Geringer JL. *Mil Med.* Published online March 10, 2026. doi:10.1093/milmed/usag099. PMID:41806383.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41806383/

## Comment 4 — manuscript p. 4 | lines 4–14 and 23–49; manuscript p. 8/Table 1 | lines 17–46

The evidence-verification and feedback content is not clearly novel relative to already-published DEFT-AI guidance. Existing literature asks supervisors to identify what the learner told the AI, determine what information the AI did and did not have, reconstruct the learner’s plan independently of the AI, determine what the AI added, verify recommendations, and specify how AI should be used on the next task. The submitted Table 1 asks substantially parallel questions about prompts, limitations, missed information, reference verification, stakes, and what the learner would do differently. This is useful educational content, but utility alone is not sufficient evidence of novelty when a nearly contemporaneous JGME publication already operationalized the same supervision sequence. Please provide a side-by-side comparison with prior DEFT-AI supervision tools and specify which prompts, decisions, or educational mechanisms in Table 1 are genuinely new.

**Relevant literature:** Preiksaitis C. *J Grad Med Educ.* 2026;18(1):12-15. doi:10.4300/JGME-D-25-01133.1. Abdulnour REE, Gin B, Boscardin CK. *N Engl J Med.* 2025;393:786-797. doi:10.1056/NEJMra2503232.

## Comment 5 — manuscript p. 5 | lines 30–57; manuscript p. 6 | lines 4–19

The manuscript treats DEFT-AI as sufficient for protecting against deskilling/never-skilling within routine encounters, but the current JGME discussion has already identified an important limitation that this paper does not address: longitudinal erosion of reasoning may accumulate across encounters and remain invisible to any single supervisory interaction. In the August 2026 JGME exchange on “Making Clinical Reasoning Visible During Resident Use of Artificial Intelligence,” the response explicitly notes that DEFT-AI operates within one supervised encounter whereas deskilling and never-skilling can develop across repeated encounters below the resolution of any single observation. This is a more advanced conceptual problem than simply mapping DEFT-AI onto OMP. The manuscript should engage with this limitation and describe how programs would detect longitudinal changes in independent reasoning, not only whether an individual encounter contains appropriate disclosure and verification.

**Relevant literature:** Kundavaram R, Dhingra B. *J Grad Med Educ.* 2026;18(4):505-506. doi:10.4300/JGME-D-26-00408.1. Preiksaitis C. *J Grad Med Educ.* 2026;18(4):507. doi:10.4300/JGME-D-26-00715.1.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42603008/
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42602992/

## Comment 6 — manuscript p. 2 | lines 43–53; manuscript p. 5 | lines 14–44

The manuscript repeatedly asserts an “expertise gap” in which learners are more technically adept or “more skilled” than supervising faculty. Facility, frequency of use, AI literacy, prompt fluency, clinical judgment, and supervisory competence are not interchangeable constructs. The authors’ own military GME survey found low attending role-modeling and substantial trainee demand for formal instruction, but it did not establish that trainees were more competent than faculty at evaluating AI output or supervising AI-mediated reasoning. Please distinguish frequency/familiarity from validated skill and either cite comparative competency data or soften “more skilled” to a narrower, supportable statement. This distinction matters because the manuscript simultaneously argues that faculty without technical expertise can safely supervise learner AI use.

## Comment 7 — manuscript p. 8/Table 1 | lines 10–16

OpenEvidence® is the only named commercial AI platform in Table 1, yet the manuscript gives no rationale for selecting it, no description of which OpenEvidence capabilities are relevant to the framework, and no explanation of whether the teaching prompts generalize to general-purpose LLMs, retrieval-augmented clinical tools, ambient scribes, or other AI systems. That distinction now matters empirically: OpenEvidence is a retrieval-augmented clinical question-answering platform with explicit biomedical citations, and its failure modes and verification workflow differ from those of unconstrained general-purpose LLMs. If the intent is a platform-agnostic supervisory framework, replace the branded example with a generic prompt or explicitly explain why OpenEvidence is pedagogically illustrative. If the product is retained, consider whether the manuscript should state that naming it is illustrative rather than an endorsement and discuss its distinct information-retrieval properties.

**Relevant literature:** Patel N, et al. *Cureus.* 2025;17(1):e76867. doi:10.7759/cureus.76867. PMID:39897335. Recent systematic review: *npj Digital Medicine* (2026), “OpenEvidence clinical question-answering platform: systematic review of early evaluations.”
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39897335/

## Comment 8 — manuscript p. 4 | lines 53–60; manuscript p. 5 | lines 4–7; manuscript p. 6 | lines 23–46

The authors’ institution already has empirical and preprint work that points toward a more substantive contribution than the current conceptual mapping. Hemmer is a coauthor of the August 2026 JMIR preprint *How Medical Students Use Artificial Intelligence for Learning: A Quality Improvement Evaluation*, which reports that 175/191 first-year students (91.6%) used AI, that most perceived gains in study efficiency and understanding, and that 22.2% rarely or never verified AI-generated information. This is directly relevant to the manuscript’s emphasis on verification and could support a measurable educational target for DEFT-AI. Hemmer was also a coauthor of the 2024 JMIR study—initially posted as a JMIR preprint—evaluating ChatGPT 3.5 for scoring free-text medical-student clinical notes. These studies demonstrate that the authors’ institution has access to concrete learner behavior, assessment tasks, and AI implementation questions. A stronger paper would test whether DEFT-AI/OMP changes verification behavior, independent reasoning, supervisor confidence, or learner outcomes rather than proposing another conceptual alignment.

## Comment 9 — manuscript p. 4 | lines 32–42

The “cyborg” versus “centaur” discussion is presented as a practical basis for assigning tighter human-AI integration to high-risk or diagnostically uncertain situations and division of labor to lower-risk tasks. This recommendation needs much more precise justification. Risk level alone does not determine whether tight integration is safer; the appropriate human-AI interaction depends on the tool’s function, evidence source, calibration, data access, task structure, failure modes, and whether the human can independently detect errors. A high-stakes task may warrant less AI delegation rather than more tightly coupled AI use. Please avoid presenting these metaphors as if they constitute validated supervisory categories unless the cited source provides evidence for the particular risk-based assignment proposed here.

## Comment 10 — manuscript p. 6 | lines 36–46

The conclusion states that aligning DEFT-AI with OMP “embeds AI literacy” into familiar workflows and advances competencies across multiple frameworks. The manuscript does not demonstrate embedding, competency acquisition, behavior change, retention, transfer, or patient-safety outcomes; it proposes a set of questions. Please distinguish a conceptual prompt set from an implemented curriculum. At minimum, the conclusion should state that the mapping *may provide a way to prompt discussion of AI use* rather than claiming that it embeds AI literacy. Preferably, the authors should prospectively test feasibility, uptake, learner reasoning, verification behavior, supervisor confidence, and unintended consequences before making implementation claims.

## Minor Comments

1. **Reference attribution:** manuscript p. 3 lines 36–39 attributes DEFT-AI extension to reference 6, but reference 6 is the 2022 DEFT/OMP article and predates DEFT-AI. The actual DEFT-AI source appears to be reference 7.
2. **Reference 2:** “Colleges. AoAM.” is not an acceptable organizational-author citation for the Association of American Medical Colleges and should be corrected.
3. **Reference 3:** the DOI is formatted as `doi:doi:10.1056/AIp2400611`.
4. **Terminology:** use “One-Minute Preceptor” consistently rather than alternating “One Minute Preceptor” and “one-minute preceptor.”
5. **Grammar:** “within real-time clinical workflows constraints” should be revised.
6. **Grammar:** “streamlined adaption” should be “streamlined adaptation.”
7. **Table 1:** “Correcting Mistakes) or Identify areas for Improvement)” contains unmatched parentheses and inconsistent capitalization.
8. **Table 1:** the D domain is called “Diagnosis, Definition, Discourse” in the table but “diagnosis, discussion, and discourse” in the text; standardize the construct.
9. **Scope:** distinguish AI scribes, evidence-retrieval systems, and generative reasoning/chat systems rather than treating “AI” as one homogeneous intervention.
10. **Claims:** wherever the manuscript says the framework “ensures,” “embeds,” or imposes little/no added burden, revise to language proportionate to a nonevaluated Perspective.

---

## Suggested Editorial Disposition

**Reject in current form.** The problem is important, but the manuscript’s principal contribution overlaps substantially with (1) the lineage of DEFT from the OMP, (2) the 2025 DEFT-AI clinical-supervision framework, and especially (3) JGME’s own February 2026 article operationalizing DEFT-AI in under two minutes with closely overlapping supervisory prompts. A revision confined to wording, citation updates, or an expanded table would not resolve the central novelty problem. A substantially different manuscript could become valuable if it reports prospective implementation data showing that an OMP-integrated DEFT-AI intervention measurably improves verification behavior, independent reasoning, faculty supervision, learner outcomes, feasibility, or longitudinal detection of deskilling.
