# Training Through Turbulence: Why Medical Education Must Build Intentionally Supportive, Historically Grounded Learning Cultures

The current moment in medical education is marked by compounding pressures: lingering effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, workforce shortages, financial strain, and ongoing moral distress associated with providing care in stretched-thin systems1,2. Political polarization, societal inequities, and rising mistrust in science further heighten stress for both clinicians and learners. These realities intensify the emotional and moral challenges of learning to care – and can make the humanistic ideals that drew many to medicine feel out of reach3-7.

Our humanistic ideals remain critical, central, and immutable, as is our duty to uphold them even in the face of the current political and geopolitical climate. A foundation of universal values, which requires institutional/organizational support, can foster clinicians’ ability to lead with ethical, professional, and moral integrity even during challenging circumstances8. Although it appears intuitive, trainees need explicit reminders to take care of the patient in front of you, do what you can, don’t dwell on what you can’t.

Patient-centered care is a prerequisite that must be prioritized based on patient needs, clinical indication, legal permissibility, and what can be realistically achieved. Faculty must model and teach patient-centered reasoning, ensuring that trainees are not managing uncertainty alone and know when to escalate issues to legal, ethics, or risk management teams. The impact of organizational decisions on patient care should be transparently communicated, and moral distress should be acknowledged as treatment and care responsibility is shared across teams. Intentionally creating these reflective and supportive spaces that center around the experience of care providers (e.g., Balint groups) is key as clinicians strive to deliver the best care for their patients.

In this moment of profound strain, the path forward lies not in denying the hardships facing clinicians and trainees but in meeting them with clarity, solidarity, and purpose. Below, we offer suggestions for navigating these tumultuous times, using our humanistic values as the central guiding principle.

## Key recommendations

### Provide historical perspective

As reflected in their responses to epidemics, new illnesses, wartime medicine, and scarcity of resources, most physicians, be they learners or teachers, ultimately navigate a path out of darkness. Current dilemmas are shaped by context and systemic challenges – not an individual failing on any one trainee’s part9-11. Current trainees may not have this longitudinal perspective and would likely benefit from historical recollections and narratives for assurance and reminders that “this too shall pass.” In the absence of this historical anchoring, modeling resilience, and authentic disclosure on the part of mentors, trainees remain vulnerable to the fallout of their own high expectations. Some trainees even seek out geographical relocation to escape the angst of value misalignment.

### Acknowledge uncertainty, validate stress, and balance emotional toll with hope

The history of medicine is rich with examples of resilience. An honest appraisal of current barriers and the relentless nature of recent events that have inhibited recovery of stressed healthcare systems is needed. This conception must be balanced with hope that is empirically grounded in the history of medicine: many such “unprecedented times” have occurred, and clinicians have persevered through them. Creating a community that upholds the tenets of psychological safety can foster networks and connections in times when trainees feel alone and isolated12. Faculty can create a safe space for learners to reflect on challenges, process their emotional responses to difficult experiences, explore perceived limitations, and debrief when current barriers to care adversely impact the patient. A safe space also promotes well-being and the sense of being valued.

### Senior clinicians should model and articulate how they are preserving their values and ideals in current times

Mentors and educators are essential for guiding and shaping the careers of future clinicians, understanding their growth from a developmental and trauma-aware perspective and fostering professional identity formation, especially as it is impacted by the current learning environment13,14. Authentically sharing historical perspectives, past experiences, and current challenges in decision making and learning can reduce the sense of isolation that trainees may feel that they are the only ones struggling with these conflicted feelings. Faculty should demonstrate professionalism, and that ethical practice and principles of patient-centered care remain central to day-to-day clinical decision making. Such modeling can be uplifting and even inspiring15.

### Discuss the historical evolution of organizational cultures in academic medicine

Throughout history, the most transformative growth has resulted when structural changes at organizational level have fostered the clinician’s ability to deliver humanistic care under extreme duress. Lessons learned from wartime and pandemic crises that led to forced systemic solutions highlight the importance of supportive and communicative organizational cultures rather than individual fortitude16. While individual resilience and mentorship are indispensable, they cannot flourish in a vacuum. The emotional labor expected of clinicians and trainees today is inseparable from the environment in which they work. Cultivating these humanistic attributes in organizations is equally important as in individuals to counter the negative effects of healthcare evolution17.

### Emphasize the critical importance of institutional support to maintain the patient-provider alliance

Support from leadership can range from regular check-ins individually to debriefing as a group with a focus on promoting moral agency, teamwork, and resilience-building activities. Despite external pressures and the outward-facing measures they must take, it is incumbent on institutions to ensure that institutional values remain aligned with and committed to patient-centered care.

To translate these conceptual values into practice, Table 1 offers practical guidance and strategies that can be translated and tailored across specialties.

## Conclusion

Medicine has always evolved through adversity. Even in these uncertain times, with the guiding principle of patient-centered care and a community united around this principle, we can both withstand and transcend the pressures of the present.
