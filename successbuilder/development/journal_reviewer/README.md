# JGME Reviewer Summary

This document summarizes two peer-review assignments completed for the *Journal of Graduate Medical Education* (JGME). In both cases, Matt Porter served as **Reviewer #1**, submitted structured review comments, and the manuscripts were ultimately **not accepted for publication** by the journal.

<details>
<summary><strong>Purpose of this summary</strong></summary>

This summary is designed for professional review by a non-technical audience. It explains, in plain language, what each manuscript was about, what Reviewer #1 evaluated, and how the review work reflected skills in critical reading, methodological assessment, evidence checking, and written feedback.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Reviewer assignment 1: JGME-D-26-00298</strong></summary>

**Manuscript title:** *Generative Artificial Intelligence Use in Pediatric Residency Personal Statements*.

**Reviewer role:** Matt Porter served as **Reviewer #1** for this manuscript and submitted both general comments and numbered, manuscript-specific critiques for the authors and editors.

**What the manuscript examined:** The paper evaluated whether pediatric residency personal statements may have reflected use of generative AI, and whether commercial AI-detection tools could be used to measure that reliably.[1]

**What Reviewer #1 evaluated:**
- Whether the manuscript's definitions were precise, especially its use of the terms “AI,” “generative AI,” and “ChatGPT.”
- Whether the factual background was accurate, including publication timelines and the broader AI landscape relevant to graduate medical education.
- Whether the selected AI-detection tools were justified, appropriately described, and supported by the literature cited by the authors.
- Whether the study design accounted for false positives, baseline noise, and meaningful analytical thresholds before drawing conclusions about increasing AI use.
- Whether the paper's conclusions were proportionate to the reliability limits of the tools used in the study.

**Reviewer #1 contributions:**
- Identified a factual error in the manuscript's timeline by noting that GPT-4 was released in March 2023, not 2022, and explained why that distinction mattered to the study's year-by-year framing.
- Flagged the manuscript's use of “AI (i.e., ChatGPT)” as overly broad and potentially misleading, and recommended clearer distinction between a general technology category and a specific product.
- Questioned the methodological basis for using “any amount of AI detection” as evidence of AI-assisted writing when the manuscript's own baseline year contained notable false positives.
- Recommended stronger engagement with the actual tools and systems available to applicants and programs, including widely used platforms and institutional infrastructure already present in the field.
- Pressed for threshold-based or sensitivity-based reanalysis so the paper's conclusions would better reflect the limitations of the underlying detectors.


**Journal outcome:** The editor decided **not to publish** the manuscript.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Reviewer assignment 2: JGME-D-25-01151</strong></summary>

**Manuscript title:** *Algorithmic Scheduling to Improve Resident Well-Being and Equity in Call Distribution*.

**Reviewer role:** Matt Porter served as **Reviewer #1** for this manuscript and submitted general comments followed by numbered, line-specific review points for the authors.

**What the manuscript examined:** The paper described a residency scheduling approach that used open-source optimization software to improve fairness and reduce administrative burden in call scheduling.

**What Reviewer #1 evaluated:**
- Whether the manuscript accurately described the authors' technical contribution versus the use of an existing open-source solver.
- Whether the methods were reproducible, including software versions, installation details, computational environment, constraints, and weighting logic.
- Whether fairness metrics such as Gini and Temporal Gini were sufficiently explained for readers outside optimization or quantitative operations research.
- Whether claims about improvement, significance, efficiency, and generalizability were fully supported by the reported data.
- Whether the discussion and conclusions properly accounted for trade-offs, including fairness versus individualized accommodation.

**Examples of Reviewer #1 contributions:**
- Distinguished between “developing software” and implementing an existing Google OR-Tools CP-SAT solver for a specific residency scheduling use case, helping clarify the manuscript's actual contribution.
- Requested concrete reproducibility details such as Python version, OR-Tools version, installation pathway, hardware or operating environment, and dependency specifics.
- Asked for numerical constraint definitions and weighted optimization details so other programs could realistically reproduce or evaluate the scheduling model.
- Identified places where the paper overstated statistical significance or broad applicability beyond what the data appeared to support.
- Highlighted a negative survey result related to personal needs accommodation and recommended reframing it as a limitation rather than glossing over it.

**Professional skills demonstrated in this review:**
- Reproducibility analysis for software-supported research.
- Technical reading across optimization, workflow, and implementation claims.
- Careful alignment of conclusions with actual results.
- Constructive written critique aimed at improving transparency and methodological rigor.

**Journal outcome:** The editor decided **not to publish** the manuscript.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>What Reviewer #1 means in this context</strong></summary>

In journal peer review, “Reviewer #1” is an anonymous designation used by the editorial system. It indicates that the reviewer provided one of the formal review reports considered by the editors during the publication decision process.

In these two assignments, Reviewer #1's role included evaluating the manuscripts' clarity, factual accuracy, methodological strength, and whether the conclusions were justified by the evidence presented.
