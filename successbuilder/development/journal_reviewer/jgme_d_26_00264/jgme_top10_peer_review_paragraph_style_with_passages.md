# Peer Review Comments: *Match Trends in Competitive Surgical Specialties Post ACGME Merger*

Comment 1  
Page 4, Lines 11-14

The abstract’s Methods statement indicates that the data were collected during the 2025 academic year, whereas the main Methods section later states that data collection occurred between July 2024 and December 2024, creating a basic but important inconsistency in the study timeline.[file:154] Because the manuscript’s framing depends heavily on its position “five years post-ACGME merger,” the exact study period needs to be stated consistently across the abstract and body so that readers can understand whether the analysis reflects a 2024 cross-sectional roster review, a 2025 academic-year snapshot, or some combination of the two. This section would therefore be stronger if the authors reconciled the abstract and methods language, explicitly defined the observation window, and clarified whether “2025 academic year” refers to resident class year rather than calendar-year data collection. This is especially important in merger-impact studies that define premerger and postmerger cohorts precisely; for example, Soliman et al. wrote that applicants were “grouped on matching in 2015-2019 (premerger) or 2020-2024 (postmerger),” showing exactly how the analytic eras were operationalized in relation to the merger.[file:154][web:192]

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Applicants were grouped on matching in 2015-2019 (premerger) or 2020-2024 (postmerger).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).

Comment 2  
Page 5, Lines 1-10

The introduction currently combines historical background, policy rationale, and claims about the benefits of the single accreditation system into one dense paragraph, and some of the wording reads more like institutional advocacy than neutral scientific framing.[file:154] Statements such as the merger “streamlines graduate medical education,” “expands opportunities,” and “fosters greater collaboration” may reflect the intended goals of accreditation unification, but the empirical literature suggests that post-merger outcomes for osteopathic applicants have been mixed across specialties rather than uniformly beneficial. This section would be more balanced if the authors explicitly attributed these benefit statements to ACGME or other institutional sources and then contrasted them with published outcome data showing specialty-specific heterogeneity. That recommendation is supported by studies showing persistent disparities after unification; for example, Kroin et al. reported that “Percent of matched:applied for DO applicants was lower than MD applicants in the fields of NSGY (p < 0.001), ENT (p < 0.001), Plastic Surgery (p < 0.001), General Surgery (p < 0.001), and Thoracic Surgery (p = 0.011),” which directly undercuts any implication that opportunity expanded evenly across competitive surgical specialties.[file:154][web:193]

Supporting source passage: Kroin et al., *The Effect of Single Accreditation on Medical Student Match Rates* (2021): “Percent of matched:applied for DO applicants was lower than MD applicants in the fields of NSGY (p < 0.001), ENT (p < 0.001), Plastic Surgery (p < 0.001), General Surgery (p < 0.001), and Thoracic Surgery (p = 0.011).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33968513/).

Comment 3  
Page 5, Lines 18-23

The manuscript states that this study offers a “unique perspective” and is the “most recent assessment” of post-merger effects, but those claims are not yet well situated within the current literature.[file:154] There is now a growing body of published work examining DO versus MD match rates and representation in competitive specialties after the merger, and without directly comparing the current study to that literature, the novelty claim feels overstated. This paragraph would be more persuasive if the authors specified what is actually distinctive about their design—such as the five-year post-merger time point, the regional breakdown, or the use of PGY1-versus-PGY5 composition as a proxy measure—and then explicitly contrasted that contribution with prior studies so that readers can see what gap is being filled. That need is reinforced by Soliman et al., who already concluded that “Postmerger, there was an increase in osteopathic students and physicians filling general surgery residency, vascular, and hand surgery fellowship positions. However, the proportion of osteopathic students who matched into integrated surgery residencies remained the same,” which shows that at least some of the manuscript’s central substantive terrain has already been studied in a modern post-merger dataset.[file:154][web:192]

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Postmerger, there was an increase in osteopathic students and physicians filling general surgery residency, vascular, and hand surgery fellowship positions. However, the proportion of osteopathic students who matched into integrated surgery residencies remained the same.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).

Comment 4  
Page 6, Lines 1-5

The study aim uses the language of “trends” and suggests that the manuscript is evaluating changes in DO representation five years after the merger, but the actual design appears to be cross-sectional, comparing PGY1 and PGY5 cohorts observed at a single time point on public residency websites.[file:154] That design can describe differences in current class composition, but it cannot establish longitudinal trends with the same strength as studies that compare match outcomes across multiple years or track applicants over time. The wording here should therefore be tightened so that the manuscript clearly states that it is using contemporaneous PGY1 and PGY5 representation as a proxy for recent differences in resident composition, rather than implying that it directly measures temporal change attributable to the merger. This distinction matters because merger-impact studies that truly evaluate trends generally use serial annual data and explicit pre/post comparisons; for example, Kroin et al. analyzed 2018 to 2020 NRMP data and reported that “From the year 2018 to 2020, the DO applicants saw a decrease of 3% in the total number of matched postgraduate year 1 spots in surgical specialties,” which is methodologically quite different from comparing resident classes within one observed year.[file:154][web:193]

Supporting source passage: Kroin et al., *The Effect of Single Accreditation on Medical Student Match Rates* (2021): “From the year 2018 to 2020, the DO applicants saw a decrease of 3% in the total number of matched postgraduate year 1 spots in surgical specialties.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33968513/).

Comment 5  
Page 6, Lines 11-24

The methods section states that degree data were collected from public residency program websites, but it does not provide enough information about how the authors handled incomplete rosters, ambiguous degree designations, outdated websites, or inconsistently formatted resident listings.[file:154] This matters because program websites are highly variable in quality and update frequency, and studies that rely on website abstraction for residency demographics typically describe detailed data-extraction rules and quality-control procedures so readers can judge the reliability of the dataset. The methods would therefore be substantially stronger if the authors explained how missing data were handled, whether more than one reviewer abstracted data, how disagreements were resolved, and what criteria were used when degree type or PGY status was unclear. The need for this level of detail is supported by website-based demographic studies such as the orthopaedic resident analysis, which defined its purpose explicitly as investigating “differences in the demographic characteristics of orthopaedic surgery residents in 2021 in regard to sex, degree type, and race/ethnicity,” thereby signaling a structured abstraction framework rather than a simple website scrape without methodological elaboration.[file:154][web:158]

Supporting source passage: Ames et al., *Journal of Orthopaedic Experience & Innovation* (2023): “The purpose of this study was to investigate differences in the demographic characteristics of orthopaedic surgery residents in 2021 in regard to sex, degree type, and race/ethnicity.” [Article](https://journaloei.scholasticahq.com/article/57307-the-demographic-make-up-of-orthopaedic-surgery-residents-in-the-united-states).

Comment 6  
Page 7, Lines 5-8

The statistical methods section contains what appears to be a substantive wording error, stating that DO representation was compared for “PGY5 and PGY5,” which almost certainly should read “PGY5 and PGY1.”[file:154] More importantly, the choice of a paired t-test across nine AAMC regions for bounded percentage data that include zero values is not adequately justified, particularly when some region-specialty cells are likely very small. Similar match-trend and demographic studies often rely on simpler categorical comparisons or descriptive analyses rather than heavily parametric inference when the data are sparse, so the current approach needs either stronger statistical justification or a more cautious presentation. This section would therefore be more convincing if the authors corrected the PGY wording, explicitly defined the unit of analysis, reported raw counts alongside percentages, and either justified the assumptions underlying the paired t-test or considered alternative methods. That concern is reinforced by Soliman et al., who used a simpler framework and wrote that “Statistical analysis using the Chi-Square test was performed to determine if there was a significant difference in the mean number of matches in the pre and postmerger groups (p < 0.05),” demonstrating a more transparent alignment between data structure and inferential method.[file:154][web:192]

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Statistical analysis using the Chi-Square test was performed to determine if there was a significant difference in the mean number of matches in the pre and postmerger groups (p < 0.05).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).

Comment 7  
Page 8, Lines 22-29

The Urology subsection contains a clear specialty-label error, stating that “DO urology residents account for 10% of all PGY5 orthopedic surgery residents,” and a similar issue appears elsewhere when general surgery data are described as ENT data.[file:154] These may seem like copyediting issues, but in a results-heavy manuscript built around specialty-specific comparisons, repeated labeling errors can undermine confidence in the accuracy of the analysis itself. This section would benefit from a careful line-by-line proofreading pass to verify that every specialty label, percentage, and narrative interpretation correctly matches the underlying table and figure data. Precision is especially important because literature in competitive fields is already documenting sensitive DO-versus-MD differences; for example, DeFroda et al. reported that “Among first-time applicants, MDs (93.1%) matched at a significantly higher rate than DOs/IMGs (68.6%, p < 0.0001),” meaning that even small reporting errors in specialty-specific manuscripts can distort readers’ understanding of already unequal match landscapes.[file:154][web:173]

Supporting source passage: DeFroda et al., *JBJS Open Access* (2023): “Among first-time applicants, MDs (93.1%) matched at a significantly higher rate than DOs/IMGs (68.6%, p < 0.0001).” [Article](https://journals.lww.com/jbjsoa/fulltext/2023/09000/a_comparison_of_match_rates_to_orthopaedic_surgery.9.aspx).

Comment 8  
Page 9, Lines 4-10

The discussion opens by concluding that the findings “suggest limited benefits of the ACGME merger” and “challenge the ACGME’s assertion that the merger enhanced opportunities,” but those claims extend beyond what the current design can support.[file:154] This study evaluates resident composition at a single point in time using program websites; it does not directly examine applicant-level match rates, selection processes, pre-merger baselines, or causal mechanisms. Published merger-impact studies show a more nuanced picture, with gains for DO representation in some areas and persistent disparities in others, suggesting that the effects of the merger are complex and specialty-specific rather than easily summarized as beneficial or not beneficial. The discussion would therefore be stronger if it reframed these findings as descriptive evidence of persistent heterogeneity in representation across specialties and regions, while acknowledging that broader causal claims about the merger itself require longitudinal match data and a different study design. That caution is supported by the PM&R analysis concluding that “These trends suggest the merger influenced application and match dynamics but coincided with other shifts in medical education,” which is a far more appropriately restrained interpretation of post-merger observational data.[file:154][web:181]

Supporting source passage: Svec et al., *PM&R* (2025): “These trends suggest the merger influenced application and match dynamics but coincided with other shifts in medical education.” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41248255/).

Comment 9  
Page 10, Lines 14-26

The limitations section appropriately notes that only five surgical specialties were included, but it does not yet address several of the manuscript’s most important methodological constraints, including reliance on public websites, possible outdated or incomplete resident rosters, exclusion of programs with missing data, lack of applicant-level match information, instability of small regional percentages, and the inability of cross-sectional PGY comparisons to establish causality.[file:154] These are not minor issues, because they materially shape how confidently readers can interpret the reported differences across specialties and regions. The limitations paragraph would therefore be much stronger if it explicitly named these data-source and design constraints, acknowledged that large percentage swings may reflect very small denominators in some cells, and clarified that the study describes representation patterns rather than verified applicant opportunity or true longitudinal trend data. This recommendation is consistent with the tone of geographic workforce literature that stresses interpretive caution; for example, Daniels et al. wrote that “Geographic access to orthopaedic surgery remains limited in nonmetropolitan regions, where over half of US counties lack an orthopaedic surgeon,” a conclusion that is carefully bounded to the observable workforce data rather than extended to stronger causal claims.[file:154][web:194]

Supporting source passage: Daniels et al., *JBJS Open Access* (2025): “Geographic access to orthopaedic surgery remains limited in nonmetropolitan regions, where over half of US counties lack an orthopaedic surgeon.” [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12674144/).

Comment 10  
Page 17-18, Table 2

Table 2 is central to the manuscript’s argument about regional representation, but it reports only percentages without raw MD and DO counts, which makes it difficult to determine how stable or meaningful many of the reported differences actually are.[file:154] This is especially important in specialties such as plastic surgery and urology, where a modest shift in one or two residents could produce a seemingly large percentage change. Many studies of residency representation and match outcomes report both counts and proportions precisely to avoid overinterpretation of small-cell variation. Table 2 would therefore be much more informative if the authors added raw numerators and denominators for each region-specialty cell, and ideally also provided a supplemental appendix with program-level or regional counts. The importance of count-based interpretation is illustrated by Soliman et al., who reported absolute and proportional changes together—for example, “Postmerger, the proportion of osteopathic students matching into general surgery residency increased significantly (DO, 5.77% vs 14.25%, p = 0.032)”—thereby allowing readers to evaluate both the direction and magnitude of change in context.[file:154][web:192]

Supporting source passage: Soliman et al., *Journal of Surgical Education* (2025): “Postmerger, the proportion of osteopathic students matching into general surgery residency increased significantly (DO, 5.77% vs 14.25%, p = 0.032).” [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40068372/).
