 Short Title:                          How Programs Use the ACGME Milestones 2.0

 Article Type:                         Original Research

 Keywords:                             Milestones; competency-based medical education; Assessement; Accreditation

 Abstract:                             Background

                                       The Milestones 2.0 assessment framework was implemented by the Accreditation
                                       Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) to support competency-based
                                       assessment of trainees.

                                       Objective

                                       This study sought to understand how the Milestones are being used by Graduate
                                       Medical Education (GME) programs nationally.

                                       Methods

                                       A validated survey instrument collecting respondent demographics, perceptions of the
                                       Milestones, and information regarding their use and implementation was distributed to
                                       Graduate Medical Education (GME) stakeholders nationally from January to March of
                                       2025. Following a computer-assisted deductive analysis that used published, core
                                       principles of Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) as initial codes, the
                                       authors completed an inductive thematic content analysis of responses.

                                       Results

                                       The survey received a total of 2,567 responses, including 1,306 complete responses.
                                       Due to the national nature of the survey, an exact response rate cannot be calculated.
                                       Most responses came from program directors (N=843) and non-physician
                                       administrative stakeholders (N=403). Based on the qualitative analysis, 13 themes
                                       were identified across the five core components of CBME: outcome competencies,
                                       tailored learning experiences, sequenced progression, competency-focused coaching,
                                       and programmatic assessment. While many themes emphasized use of the Milestones
                                       to support CBME principles, several practical and conceptual concerns emerged
                                       around the use of milestone data.

                                       Conclusions

                                       The themes that emerged through this analysis demonstrate that Milestones 2.0
                                       provides an assessment framework for programs that aligns with the core CBME
                                       principles. At the same time, further work is needed to ensure that faculty and trainees
                                       can use the Milestones effectively and that determinations of competence correlate
                                       with practical clinical outcomes.

12   Introduction
13
14   Educational Milestones were implemented for the assessment of graduate medical trainees by the
15
16
17   Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) in 2013.1,2 One goal of these
18
19   milestones was to continue the shift in trainee assessment toward a competency-based medical
20
21
     education (CBME) framework.3,4 The Milestones assess trainees along an expected developmental
22
23
24   trajectory across the six core competencies: interpersonal and communication skills, medical
25
26   knowledge, patient care, practice-based learning and improvement, professionalism, and systems-
27
28
29   based practice.
30
31
32
33
34   After the initial Milestones were developed, the ACGME underwent an iterative review process,
35
36   creating a revised set of milestones termed “Milestones 2.0.”2 Milestone data are reported to the
37
38
39
     ACGME nationally for accreditation, but little is known about how programs use these data to
40
41   operationalize CBME in practice. The core components of CBME5 provide a framework for
42
43   examining how Milestones inform: outcome competencies, sequenced progression, tailored
44
45
46   learning experiences, competency-focused coaching, and programmatic assessment. This study
47
48   analyzed the results from a national survey of stakeholders in Graduate Medical Education (GME)
49
50
51   to understand how the Milestones 2.0 framework is being used to promote the core components of
52
53   CBME.

 4   Survey Design and Distribution
 5
 6
 7   The survey on which this study is based was developed by Laura Hirshfield of the University of
 8
 9   Illinois Chicago and the ACGME Milestones development team. The initial phase of their
10
11
12   development was based on interviews with graduate medical education leaders (program directors,
13
14   Clinical Competency Committee [CCC] chairs, etc.) across ACGME-accredited specialties. These
15
16
17   interviews were conducted by trained interviewers, recorded, and transcribed; an inductive
18
19   analysis of the transcripts was then used to develop the final survey. The full methods used in the
20
21
     development of this survey are concurrently reviewed and published alongside this article (L. E.
22
23
24   Hirshfield, P. B. Barlow, I. Haynes, S. McLean, L. Edgar, unpublished manuscript, 2026) . The
25
26   final iteration of the survey contained 34 questions, including multiple-choice, Likert-scale, and
27
28
29   open-ended, narrative items. The survey was distributed via Qualtrics XM (Qualtrics, Provo, UT)
30
31   to ACGME-affiliated stakeholders nationally from January 30 – March 30, 2025.

36   Data Analysis
37
38
39
     Descriptive data regarding response rates and respondent demographics were summarized using
40
41   STATA Version 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). The authors reviewed the survey and
42
43   coded each question according to the CBME component(s) it addressed to ensure there was
44
45
46   comprehensive representation of all core components. The core components framework is from
47
48   published work by Van Melle et al and includes: outcome competencies, sequenced progression,
49
50
51   tailored learning experiences, competency-focused coaching, and programmatic assessment.5
52
53   Given the volume of qualitative data, Computer-Assisted Qualitative Data Analysis (CAQDAS)
54
55
56   using MAXQDA 24 (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany) was used for initial data coding. Based
57
58
59
60
61

 7   established core components of CBME from the literature as the initial codes.5

12   The coded responses from the initial deductive analysis were then reviewed by the authors in pairs:
13
14   outcome competencies (SM, RM), tailored learning experiences and sequenced progression (IH,
15

17   SA), and finally competency-focused coaching and programmatic assessment (SAM, MB). Each

 4   Outcome Competencies
 5
 6
 7   Shared Outcome Expectations
 8
 9   Respondents described the Milestones as providing a shared framework for defining the
10
11
12   competencies expected of residents and fellows throughout their training. Standardized language
13
14   and developmental expectations helped faculty and learners establish a common understanding of
15
16
17   physician competence and what successful performance should look like across stages of training.
18
19   One respondent described the Milestones as “a guide that we can share with fellows and faculty to
20
21
     have common language and expectations” (12) by “help[ing] the faculty create a shared mental
22
23
24   model” (183). Respondents also valued the breadth of the framework, describing it as
25
26   encompassing competencies extending beyond medical knowledge and patient care.
27
28
29
30
31   However, respondents questioned whether these outcomes were consistently defined with
32
33
34   sufficient specificity to meaningfully represent competence. Although Milestones “describe a
35
36   broad array of competencies” (10), another described them as “too nebulous and not tied to actual
37
38
39
     observable behaviors” (24). Respondents valued a common framework for defining outcomes
40
41   while expressing uncertainty about how individual Milestones match observable clinical
42
43   outcomes.
44
45

48   Alignment with Independent Practice
49
50
51   Responses emphasized using the Milestones to evaluate learners' progression toward autonomous,
52
53   unsupervised practice and to support graduation decisions. Some viewed the breadth of the
54
55
56   Milestones as supporting this goal, noting that their “broad scopes allow programs [to] assess the
57
58   multitude of skills, qualities, and abilities needed to practice independently” (23). However,
59
60
61
62
63

65

 2
 3
 4   respondents questioned whether all Milestones “…translate well to the requirements of
 5
 6
 7   independent practice” (54). These responses highlighted a tension between the value of a
 8
 9   comprehensive framework for defining competency outcomes and concern about whether some
10
11
12   Milestones appropriately reflect the capabilities required of practicing physicians.

17   Sequenced Progression
18
19   Defining the Typical Developmental Trajectory
20
21
     Respondents described the Milestones as defining an expected developmental trajectory for
22
23
24   trainees. One respondent described the Milestones as providing "meaningful levels that clearly
25
26   differentiate between average, good and excellent trainees" (120). Others noted that they allow
27
28
29   programs to clearly "understand where the learner is in the development to independence" (125).
30
31   These responses emphasized that the framework provides a shared reference for understanding
32
33
34   learner progression, setting developmental goals, and identifying trainees who begin to “fall off
35
36   the [expected developmental] curve” (43).

41   Challenges Distinguishing Between Performance Levels
42
43   Although many respondents viewed the Milestones as clearly defining developmental progression,
44
45
46   others felt that there is “indistinct differentiation within and between levels” (98). Responses
47
48   described difficulty translating Milestone descriptors into observable behaviors, resulting in
49
50
51   inconsistent interpretation and scoring. These challenges were reported to be particularly
52
53   pronounced in non-patient-facing specialties and specialties that perceived limited alignment with
54
55
56   the harmonized Milestones. Across both of these groups of respondents, there were reports that it
57
58   was challenging for “faculty to use the milestones as a progression along a training pathway” (15).

 7   Tailored Learning Experiences
 8
 9   Learner-Centered Experiences
10
11
12   Programs used Milestone data to individualize learning experiences based on trainees' strengths,
13
14   areas for growth, and developmental goals. Respondents described using Milestones to develop
15
16
17   individualized learning plans (ILPs), tailor remediation strategies for struggling learners, and
18
19   identify trainees ready for greater autonomy, leadership opportunities, or advanced educational
20
21
     experiences. They allow CCCs to "focus on the outliers as well to provide more meaningful
22
23
24   individualized plans," (41) ensuring high-performing trainees are "offered additional experiences
25
26   and more independence" (138). To complete the sufficiently detailed assessments needed to design
27
28
29   these tailored experiences, programs have felt increased “administrative, clinical, and
30
31   documentation demands [that] contribute to burn out” (81). While programs are working to create
32
33
34   learning experiences that are learner-centric, this administrative burden can stifle these efforts.

     Competency-Focused Coaching
40

42   Individualized Learner Development
43
44
45   Respondents described incorporating Milestones into advisory meetings, coaching sessions, and
46
47   semiannual reviews to promote learner reflection, compare self-assessments with faculty
48
49
50
     evaluations, and identify strengths and areas for growth. Some programs reported assigning
51
52   trainees a "faculty advisor” (85) or “clinical coach who reviews [learners’] assessments with them”
53
54   (87). Respondents used discrepancies to guide coaching conversations and establish individualized
55
56
57   goals, for example, by “understand[ing] how (and why) discrepancies might arise between faculty
58
59   (CCC) evaluations and self-evaluations” (140).

 7   Coaching Across the Performance Spectrum
 8
 9   Programs use milestone data to tailor coaching for learners across the performance spectrum,
10
11
12   although respondents described substantially greater emphasis on remediation than on
13
14   development for high-performing trainees. One respondent explained that the Milestones allowed
15
16
17   faculty to "further drill down" (30) and identify specific developmental needs. In contrast, several
18
19   respondents found that the Milestones "generally don't provide useful feedback for high-
20
21
     performing learners" (20) and were "less helpful for residents that are overall doing well" (44).
22
23
24
25
26   Integration with Local Coaching Frameworks
27
28
29   Respondents described adapting the Milestones to local educational models that better aligned with
30
31   specialty-specific curricula and coaching processes. Examples included “creat[ing their] own
32
33
34   anchors for the milestones” (61) or mapping Milestones back onto more familiar EPAs to help
35
36   faculty with the transition to this assessment framework.

40
41   Barriers to Competency-Focused Coaching
42
43   Respondents identified three primary barriers to using Milestones for competency-focused
44
45
46   coaching: insufficient specificity, inconsistent faculty interpretation, and limited learner
47
48   engagement. Many described the Milestones as too broad to support actionable coaching because
49
50
51   they "lack sufficient granularity for meaningful feedback" (20). Variability in faculty interpretation
52
53   reduced the consistency of Milestone ratings, with one respondent describing them as "too vague
54
55
56   and subjective amongst individuals and programs" (29). Finally, respondents stated that learners
57
58   value narrative feedback and coaching conversations more than Milestone reports, with some
59
60
61

14
15
16
17   Assessment of Trainee Competence
18
19   Programs use milestone data to assess trainee competence longitudinally and support decisions

22
     regarding learner progression and graduation. Respondents described using Milestones to monitor
23

27

 4   Discussion
 5
 6
 7   CBME is an outcomes-based approach to medical training, structured around an organizing
 8
 9   framework of competencies. For GME in the United States, the organizing framework for training
10
11
12   programs is the ACGME Milestones 2.0.2,9 The expectation is that every trainee progresses through
13
14   the Milestones and ultimately achieves competence in the unsupervised care of patients and
15
16
17   families within their specialty by the completion of their training program. 6–8 Our findings
18
19   demonstrate that programs use the Milestones in ways that align with all five core components of
20
21
     CBME,5 providing faculty and residents with a shared mental model of expectations and a structure
22
23
24   for translating competency-based principles into educational practice,10 albeit with several
25
26   identified limitations.
27
28
29
30
31   The themes representing successful use of the Milestones described here align with existing
32
33
34   literature describing CBME through lenses of: learner-centered coaching and growth,11–13
35
36   progression along a performance continuum,14–17 and the use of educational data to evaluate
37
38
39
     programs and learning experiences.18–22 Our findings further demonstrate how Milestones are used
40
41   across these interconnected functions: to define shared expectations for competence, establish
42
43   developmental trajectories, assess readiness for unsupervised practice, individualize learning and
44
45
46   coaching, support progression and graduation decisions, and identify opportunities for curricular
47
48   and program improvement.
49
50
51
52
53   Throughout the analytical process, the authors noted that the identified themes often reflected
54
55
56   multiple core components of CBME. The theme of “readiness for independent practice,” as an
57
58   example, was often described in the context of the competency outcome but also informed
59
60
61
62
63
64                                                                                                  18
65
 1
 2
 3
 4   longitudinal assessment of progression, identification of learners requiring additional support, and
 5
 6
 7   tailored coaching or experiences that move trainees toward competency. Similarly, aggregate
 8
 9   Milestone data were used not only to assess individual learners but also to identify curricular gaps
10
11
12   and evaluate training programs. These findings illustrate that the core components of CBME
13
14   function as interconnected pieces rather than discrete processes. The Milestones may serve not
15
16
17   only as a required measure of outcomes, but also as an organizing framework that links these
18
19   components in educational practice.
20
21
22
23
24   While there are productive efforts to use the Milestones to support CBME, there were also multiple
25
26   reported limitations of the Milestones. Across themes, these limitations relate to uncertainty
27
28
29   regarding how faculty or trainees should use milestone data, which may contribute to decreased
30
31   confidence in Milestones 2.0 as an assessment tool. Several of these limitations appeared across
32
33
34   more than one component, suggesting that limitations in the assessment framework may constrain
35
36   the usefulness of Milestones for CBME in some circumstances. Multiple respondents did,
37
38
39
     however, distinguish support for CBME as an educational philosophy from their dissatisfaction
40
41   with their current use of the Milestones.
42
43
44
45
46   While the authors believe this analysis supports the growing literature on the benefit of the
47
48   Milestones as an assessment strategy,14,15,23 we recognize that there remain questions regarding the
49
50
51   long-term implications of Milestone-based assessment.24 Further faculty and programmatic
52
53   development at national and institutional levels is needed to guide the use and interpretation of
54
55
56   Milestones in educational practice. These efforts should, in part, focus on the Milestones as an
57
58
59
60
61
62
63
64                                                                                                    19
65
 1
 2
 3
 4   initially formative and ultimately summative assessment of clinical competence and readiness to
 5
 6
 7   practice safely without supervision.
 8
 9
10
11
12   Additionally, ongoing research efforts will be necessary to continually validate the Milestones as
13
14   markers of clinical competence. Such work should examine whether Milestone ratings are
15
16
17   associated with meaningful educational and clinical outcomes and whether their use improves
18
19   learner development or performance to address some of the concerns raised by respondents in this
20
21
     survey.25 This work should be careful not to conflate clinical competence with the notion of clinical
22
23
24   excellence, which merits its own line of research and is a nuanced discussion beyond the scope of
25
26   this paper.26
27
28
29
30
31   This work, in addition to the aforementioned, is primarily limited by the potential for interpretive
32
33
34   bias on the part of the authors and the over-generalized nature of these findings across specialties
35
36   and programs. To limit interpretive bias, authors were intentionally divided into groups to limit
37
38
39
     institutional overlap, and all themes were discussed both in author pairs and with the entire author
40
41   group to ensure thematic saturation and consistency in interpretation. As pointed out by some
42
43   respondents, the Milestones may also be applied differently in different specialties even after
44
45
46   attempts to make them widely applicable. However, this analysis lacked adequate data to fully
47
48   characterize these differences. The qualitative nature of the study also describes how stakeholders
49
50
51   understand and report using Milestones but cannot establish the frequency of these practices or
52
53   their effects on learner or program outcomes. Further work should focus on quantitative, learner-
54
55
56   and specialty-centered educational outcomes to aid in interpreting these qualitative findings and
57
58   address these limitations.

 7   Conclusions
 8
 9   This study provides further support that GME stakeholders nationally are utilizing the Milestones
10
11
12   2.0 assessment framework in alignment with the core components of CBME to establish shared
13
14   expectations for competence, characterize developmental progression, individualize learning and
15
16
17   coaching, inform progression and graduation decisions, and guide programmatic improvement.
18
19   These overlapping uses suggest that the Milestones can function as more than an outcomes-based
20
21
     assessment tool; they can provide an organizing framework through which programs
22
23
24   operationalize interconnected components of CBME. However, limitations in interpretation,
25
26   variability in use, and administrative burden may constrain their effectiveness. Further research is
27
28
29   needed to quantify these findings, and further educational development is needed to improve
30
31   stakeholder uptake of evidence-based principles of Milestone utilization.

             Introduction

             Thank you for taking the time to provide your experience with Milestones 2.0. The Milestones 2.0
             development process concluded in 2023. Efforts by the ACGME then moved to conducting a principles-
             focused program evaluation in collaboration with Dr. Laura E. Hirshfield, PhD, at University of Illinois
             Chicago (UIC). Dr. Hirshfield has developed a survey guided by the principles upon which the Milestones
             were first developed and interviews conducted with 45 stakeholders. The survey is designed to elicit your
             perspectives and experiences regarding the implementation and use of Milestones 2.0. Only aggregated
             summary results will be shared with the ACGME.

             As a user of the Milestones, we invite you to participate in helping the ACGME identify and understand how
             the Milestones are, or are not, working within your context. By keeping the survey anonymous, we strongly
             encourage you to provide your honest feedback to improve the Milestones framework to better serve the
             GME community and its learners.

             You may not be familiar with one phrase used within the survey – harmonized Milestones. Harmonized
             Milestones include a set of 11 subcompetencies across Interpersonal and Communication Skills, Practice-
             Based Learning and Improvement, Professionalism, and Systems-Based Practice that describe knowledge,
             skills, and behaviors applicable across all surgical and medical specialties. Each specialty was offered the
             opportunity to make specialty-specific edits. Few specialties made substantial changes to the harmonized
             Milestones.

             The UIC Institutional Review Board determined that this evaluation does not constitute research involving
             human subjects as defined by federal guidelines.
