## Confidential Comments to the Editor

First, the author list includes ACGME Milestones staff (here the Director of Milestone Development and a former Milestones Project Manager), the survey was developed with the ACGME Milestones team and distributed through ACGME channels, and the manuscript as provided carries no disclosure or conflict-of-interest statement. For a supplement article that concludes, favorably, that the accreditor's framework aligns with the core components of CBME, that omission needs repair before publication (Comment 4).

Second, the verbatim quotes that constitute this paper's evidence carry respondent numbers that differ between the text and Table 3 for identical quotes (Comment 8). I found four instances without looking for them systematically. The authors should be asked to audit every attributed quote against source data as a condition of resubmission. The reference list is accurate where I spot-checked it (references 5, 15, and 25 verified field for field; see Key External Sources). With the reporting and inference problems below repaired, I would expect to support publication, and I am willing to review the revision.

## General Comments to Authors

The manuscript analyzes open-ended responses from a national survey of GME stakeholders (2,567 responses, 1,306 completions) to describe how programs use Milestones 2.0, organizing the findings under the five core components of CBME from Van Melle and colleagues. A deductive pass coded the data against the five components; an inductive pass then developed 13 themes within them. The topic is squarely in this supplement's remit, the sample is one no other group is positioned to collect, the verbatim data are rich, and the limitations section is more candid than most. Table 2 contains the paper's most actionable finding: a majority of respondents report that frontline faculty do not use Milestones data at all.

The weaknesses are concentrated in inference and reporting rather than in the underlying data. The headline conclusion (that Milestones use aligns with all five core components) is substantially built into a design that sorted the data into those five components before analysis began, and the paper never shows the reader the material that fit the framework poorly or not at all. The qualitative corpus is never sized, the denominators shift between sentences, the method is named three different ways and specified in none, the respondent attributions in Table 3 disagree with the text, and there is no disclosure statement despite the authorship. All of this is repairable from data the authors already hold, and the comments below are written to be rebuilt against.

## Comment 1: Abstract, ms p. 3, lines 53-58; Methods, ms p. 5, lines 43-58 and ms p. 6, lines 5-33; Discussion, ms p. 18, lines 17-26 and ms p. 19, lines 5-19 | The deductive design sorts the data into the framework that the Conclusions then discover in it

The Methods describe a directed analysis in the strict sense: survey questions were coded to CBME components in advance (ms p. 5, line 43), and the response data were then coded using the five core components as the initial codes (ms p. 5, lines 53-58). The Abstract and Discussion conclude that the resulting themes "demonstrate that Milestones 2.0 provides an assessment framework for programs that aligns with the core CBME principles" and that "programs use the Milestones in ways that align with all five core components" (ms p. 18, line 19). A design that files every response under one of five headings cannot fail to find all five headings populated; finding them is the method, not a result. The canonical methods paper for exactly this design (Hsieh and Shannon, in the Relevant Literature) warns that directed content analysis carries a strong risk of finding evidence supportive of the theory, and prescribes the remedy: report the data that do not fit the initial categories, including text assigned new codes.

Three repairs would make the claim appraised rather than assumed. First, report the volume of coded material per component and, critically, any responses or candidate themes that fit no component or fit poorly, and what became of them. Second, resolve the internal tension between the Methods and the Discussion: the Methods state that when a theme spanned components, the authors "debated until consensus was reached on which core component most appropriately related to the theme" (ms p. 6, lines 27-33), enforcing single assignment, while the Discussion concludes that the components "function as interconnected pieces rather than discrete processes" (ms p. 19, line 14). The analysis as described forbids the phenomenon the Discussion reports. Third, temper the demonstrative verbs: on this design the data support "reported uses could be organized under all five components, with the following gaps and misfits," which is still a useful supplement finding. The thinness of the fit in places is itself informative: Tailored Learning Experiences, alone among the five components, yields a single theme (ms p. 12), and that theme ("Learner-Centered Experiences") restates the component rather than characterizing it. Saying where the framework fits badly would strengthen, not weaken, the paper's contribution.

## Comment 2: Abstract, ms p. 3, line 19; Methods, ms p. 5, lines 7-26 | The instrument is called "validated," but its development and any validity evidence rest on an unpublished methods manuscript

The Abstract describes "a validated survey instrument." The Methods state that the survey was developed from interviews with GME leaders and that "the full methods used in the development of this survey are concurrently reviewed and published alongside this article," citing an unpublished 2026 manuscript (ms p. 5, line 24). A reader of this article therefore has no access to any validity evidence: no pilot testing, no cognitive interviewing results, no item development account, nothing beyond the assertion. Either summarize the development and the validity evidence in this manuscript or its supplement (the interview sample, how items were generated and refined, any pretesting), or delete "validated" from the Abstract and describe the instrument accurately as developed for this evaluation. Relatedly, state which survey items this analysis used; at present the instrument's 34 items are mentioned (ms p. 5, lines 24-26) but the subset feeding this analysis is never identified, which also matters for Comment 3.

## Comment 3: Results, ms p. 6, line 51 and ms p. 7, lines 5-14; Tables 1 and 2 (bundle pp. 9-11) | The analysis set is never defined, the qualitative corpus is never sized, and the denominators move

This manuscript never states the size of the material actually analyzed. The Results open with 2,567 responses and 1,306 completions (ms p. 6, line 51), but the number of respondents whose open-ended data entered this thematic analysis, and the number of coded responses or comments, appear nowhere. The treatment of partial completions is likewise unstated, and the text implies they were included in some denominators: the role distribution is reported against N=1,600 (ms p. 7, line 7), a base larger than the 1,306 completers. Every percentage in the Results rides on a denominator the reader must reverse-engineer (1,600 for role, 1,675 for years in role, roughly 1,925 for program type, roughly 1,247 and 1,290 within Table 1), and the Abstract repeats the two largest role counts (N=843, N=403) with no denominator at all.

Two specific consequences need repair. First, Table 2, the paper's only quantitative result about use, rests on N=283 and N=281 respondents (ms p. 8, lines 55-58 and ms p. 9, line 4): roughly one respondent in nine answered that item. That does not disqualify the finding, which is important, but the text reports it without any caution about item nonresponse or any description of who the 283 were. Second, Table 1's program-type block is internally inconsistent as printed: Academic 1261 (65.5 percent), Rural 85 (4.4), FQHC 68 (3.5), and VA 51 (2.6) all imply a common base of about 1,925 respondents, against which Community 460 is 23.9 percent, not the printed 29.3 percent (bundle p. 10, lines 17-22). Either the count or the percentage is wrong. Please define the analysis set once, state a denominator beside every count and percentage in text, abstract, and tables, correct Table 1, and add the corpus counts (respondents and coded units) that a qualitative analysis of this kind conventionally reports.

## Comment 4: Title page, ms p. 1, lines 29-37; Methods, ms p. 5, lines 7-9; Supplement, Introduction (bundle p. 26) | Positionality and disclosure are absent for an accreditor's evaluation of its own framework

The second author-listed affiliation block identifies the Director of Milestone Development at the ACGME and a Milestones Project Manager at the ACGME as co-authors; the survey was developed by "the ACGME Milestones development team" with a university partner and distributed to the programs the ACGME accredits. The manuscript as provided contains no conflict-of-interest or disclosure statement and no positionality discussion. Insider evaluation has genuine advantages, and the authors are entitled to claim them explicitly. What readers need is the disclosure itself, a short paragraph on how author roles in Milestones development and governance were managed during analysis (the pair structure in the Methods is relevant here and could be described as the safeguard it may have been), and the protections that did exist (UIC IRB determination, the anonymity promise in the supplemental instrument, aggregated reporting) stated in the main text rather than only in the supplement's introduction. It is worth noting that the manuscript's own reference 25, a Milestones outcomes study with ACGME-affiliated authors, carries a full conflict-of-interest disclosure including ACGME employment; this manuscript should meet the standard its own reference list models.

## Comment 5: Results, ms p. 7, lines 5-14 and ms p. 8, line 55 to ms p. 9, line 4; Conclusions, ms p. 21, lines 9-31 | Leadership perceptions are reported as program practice, and the paper's hardest finding is left undiscussed

The respondents are 52.7 percent program directors and 25.2 percent non-physician administrators; residents and fellows, the subjects of the coaching and tailored-learning claims, are 50 respondents (3.1 percent), and frontline faculty are barely present as respondents at all. What the survey measures is therefore how program leadership perceives and reports Milestones use, frequently at second hand (respondents were asked whether other stakeholders use the data). The Limitations concede exactly this: the study "describes how stakeholders understand and report using Milestones but cannot establish the frequency of these practices or their effects" (ms p. 20). But the title ("How Programs Use the ACGME Milestones 2.0"), the Abstract's conclusions, and the Conclusions section (ms p. 21) state use as observed fact. Align the claims with the evidence throughout: stakeholder-reported use, predominantly as reported by program directors and administrators.

The same imbalance affects the paper's best finding. Table 2 reports that 61.6 percent of respondents answering said frontline faculty do not use Milestones 2.0 data (ms p. 9, line 4), while learners and core faculty are reported as users. If the framework does not reach the faculty who generate most assessment data, that is a first-order implementation result for this supplement, arguably the most consequential number in the paper. It receives one paragraph in the Results and no engagement in the Discussion, which foregrounds alignment instead. The Discussion should grapple with it directly: whose reports these are (directors reporting on faculty), what could produce the gap, and what it implies for the coaching and programmatic-assessment claims built on faculty-generated data.

## Comment 6: Methods, ms p. 5, lines 53-58 and ms p. 6, lines 11-33 | The qualitative method is named three ways and specified in none

Across the Abstract and Methods the analysis is called "computer-assisted deductive analysis," "thematic content analysis," and analysis conducted "through thematic content analysis" after coding in MAXQDA 24. Thematic analysis and content analysis are distinct traditions with different procedures, and the manuscript cites a methodological source for neither (Van Melle supplies the framework, not the method). What was actually done, a deductive start from theory-derived categories followed by inductive theme development within them, is recognizable as directed content analysis with a conventional second pass, and naming it, citing it, and reporting against the SRQR standard would resolve most of this comment.

Specification gaps that remain even after naming: (1) "CAQDAS using MAXQDA 24 was used for initial data coding" (ms p. 5, lines 53-56) does not say who applied codes to responses, how many coders touched the same material, or whether MAXQDA's automated functions played any role; software does not code, people do, and the sentence should say which people. (2) The pair structure assigns each author pair a disjoint set of components (ms p. 6, lines 11-24), so no two analysts ever independently coded the same component's data; the cross-checks that qualitative readers look for (calibration across analysts, an audit trail, a codebook) are undescribed, and no theme definitions or coding rules are provided beyond Table 3's quotes. (3) "Reached thematic saturation" (ms p. 6, line 26) is misapplied to a fixed, already-collected corpus: saturation describes a stopping judgment during concurrent data collection and analysis, and no new data were arriving here. Describe the actual stopping rule (for example, pairs iterated until no new themes were proposed across a stated number of review rounds). None of these requires new analysis; all of it is reporting the authors can supply.

## Comment 7: Supplement, Section 1, items 3 and 7-8 (bundle pp. 26-28); Results, ms p. 11, lines 51-58 | Specialty and CCC role were collected and never reported, although the Results make specialty-level claims

The instrument required each respondent to answer for one specific specialty or subspecialty (Supplement, Section 1, item 3) and collected detailed CCC involvement (items 7-8: membership, chair role, committee size, meeting frequency, faculty development). Neither distribution appears in the manuscript: Table 1 describes role, program type, program size, and tenure, but not the specialty mix or the CCC roles of the people reporting on CCC-centered processes. The omission matters because the Results themselves make specialty-differentiated claims, reporting that level-discrimination problems were "particularly pronounced in non-patient-facing specialties and specialties that perceived limited alignment with the harmonized Milestones" (ms p. 11, lines 51-58), a comparison the reader cannot weigh without knowing how many respondents each specialty group contributed. The Limitations note that the analysis "lacked adequate data to fully characterize these differences," which may be true at the level of theme saturation by specialty, but the descriptive distributions exist by construction and should be reported, with the specialty claim either supported by them or softened.

## Comment 8: Results text, ms p. 13, line 17 and ms p. 14, lines 26-36; Table 3 (bundle pp. 18-20) | Identical quotes carry different respondent numbers in the text and in Table 3; every attribution needs auditing

Verbatim quotes are this paper's evidence, and their attributions are inconsistent between the narrative and Table 3. Four instances, found without a systematic search:

1. "further drill down": text, respondent 30 (ms p. 13, line 17); Table 3, respondent 50.
2. "less helpful for residents that are overall doing well": text, respondent 44; Table 3, respondent 123.
3. "an objective framework with which to assess learners": text, respondent 92 (ms p. 14, lines 26-27); Table 3, respondent 119.
4. "consistent information that supports a level of competence": text, respondent 59 (ms p. 14, line 36); Table 3, respondent 87.

Any one instance could be a transcription slip; four, in both directions, indicate that the text and the table were assembled from different extracts of the data. Please audit every attributed quote in the manuscript and Table 3 against the source data, state in the Methods whether respondent numbers are stable identifiers within this analysis, and correct whichever side is wrong in each case. I treat this as a condition of support rather than a copyediting note: a qualitative paper whose quotes cannot be traced cannot be appraised.

## Minor Comments

1. **Results text (ms p. 10, line 48) vs Table 3 (bundle p. 19):** The second Outcome Competencies theme is headed "Alignment with Independent Practice" in the text and "Readiness for Independent Practice" in Table 3. Harmonize theme names across text, table, and any abstract enumeration.
2. **Manuscript header (bundle p. 1):** The keywords as submitted read "Assessement" for "Assessment." Production metadata, but it propagates to indexing; please correct.
3. **Supplemental survey, title (bundle p. 26):** The instrument is headed "Milestones 3.0 Survey" throughout, for an evaluation of Milestones 2.0; its own introduction describes experience with Milestones 2.0. If 3.0 names the project rather than the framework, say so once; otherwise retitle to avoid confusion about what was evaluated.
4. **Title page, ms p. 1, lines 3-6, and Corresponding Author block, ms p. 2 (bundle p. 4):** The author biography places the corresponding author in the Department of Health Sciences at the University of North Carolina, while the correspondence address gives the Division of Radiologic Science, School of Medicine, at the same university, and the cover letter signature lists only the adjunct appointment. Reconcile the affiliation and address so correspondence reaches the right office.
5. **Discussion, ms p. 18, lines 31-48:** The literature alignment paragraph cites references 11-22 in three dense clusters attached to one-sentence summaries ("learner-centered coaching and growth," "progression along a performance continuum"). As written the citations are decorative; either engage one or two of these findings concretely against the themes or trim the paragraph to the sources actually discussed.

## Suggested Editorial Disposition

**Major Revision.**

1. The contribution fits the supplement and is not readily replaceable: a national, stakeholder-level account of how Milestones 2.0 are actually being used, organized against the core components framework the field uses, with verbatim data and one genuinely important quantitative signal (frontline faculty non-use). The data set justifies a revision cycle rather than rejection.
2. The deficiencies are nonetheless more than cosmetic. The central conclusion is partly an artifact of the deductive design (Comment 1), the instrument's validity is asserted rather than shown (Comment 2), the analysis set and denominators are not reported well enough to appraise any number in the paper (Comment 3), the method is underspecified (Comment 6), and the quote attributions are unreliable as printed (Comment 8). A minor revision would understate all of that; a rejection would waste a data set the supplement needs.
3. Three comments are conditions of my support: Comment 1 (report framework misfit and temper the demonstrative claims, or evidence them), Comment 4 (disclosure and positionality), and Comment 8 (a complete audit of quote attributions). The remaining comments admit more than one acceptable remedy, and I have indicated the simplest in each case.
4. I would be glad to review the revision. The checks are concrete: corpus counts and per-item denominators present, Table 1 arithmetic corrected, "validated" supported or removed, disclosure added, frontline faculty finding discussed, method named and cited, specialty and CCC distributions reported, and text and Table 3 attributions in agreement.
